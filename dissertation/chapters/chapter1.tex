\chapter{Introduction}

\begin{chapterintro}
This chapter introduces the problem of unemployment and job seeking in South Africa, and asks whether and how ICT might help with some of the barriers job seekers experience. It then presents co-design, the approach used in this research to explore those barriers with job seekers and their trainers, together with the study context and participants. The chapter ends with the research questions that guided the investigation, followed by a roadmap of the chapters ahead.
\end{chapterintro}

At the time of the study, the global unemployment rate was estimated at 5.8\% in 2015 and 2016 \cite{ilo2016}, while South Africa's unemployment rate was 26.7\% in the first quarter of 2016 \cite{statssa2016q1}. More recently, Statistics South Africa reported an official unemployment rate of 33.6\% in the second quarter of 2026, representing approximately 8.5 million unemployed people \cite{statssa2026}. This represents an increase of 6.9 percentage points from the first quarter of 2016, indicating that unemployment remains a significant challenge in South Africa and reinforcing the continued relevance of this research. The United Nations listed ``Decent Work and Economic Growth'' as Goal 8 of the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), with the aim of achieving full and productive employment through economic development, technological advancement and innovation \cite{unsdg8}.

Unemployment is a broad social and economic development challenge, but some of the barriers associated with job seeking can be addressed or supported through ICT-based approaches. Unemployment is also associated with wider social and economic difficulties, including poverty, inequality, ill-health and limited access to opportunities.

In addressing unemployment and the barriers associated with job seeking, several digital employment services have been developed, with ICT solutions being used to improve access to job information, communication and employment opportunities. Among these are Ummeli, a South African digital employment service intended to support job seekers \cite{gitau2012}, and Babajob, an Indian platform developed to connect job seekers with employers \cite{babajob2016}. The reasons for discussing these applications are given in Section~3.3, \textit{ICT Interventions for Unemployment}.

Job readiness (JR, defined in Section 2.2) programmes and non-governmental organisations (NGOs) in Cape Town address aspects of unemployment through upskilling and employment preparation. However, job seekers may continue to experience barriers that can potentially be addressed or reduced through appropriate ICT solutions. JR graduates may still face issues such as poor search skills, limited access to mobile devices and the cost of data required to access job opportunities.

Co-design therefore provides an approach for working with these NGOs and job seekers to identify where ICT and digital-design expertise might play a useful role in addressing such barriers. In this research, we used co-design to understand the barriers experienced by job seekers and to explore which of these barriers could be addressed or reduced through ICT-supported solutions. By using co-design, we were able to make use of a combination of different tools and methods to engage participants, move flexibly between research and design stages, and obtain participant-oriented suggestions and possible solutions. We therefore used participant observation, interviews, one-on-one discussions and workshops as appropriate for the different enquiry and design stages.

The first cycle of our research focused on discovering and understanding our participants' capabilities and their use of communication and networking technologies for job searching and other everyday activities. In Cycle 2, we worked with participants to refine an idea for an enabling artefact intended to assist with job finding, using a PowerPoint prototype. Finally, in Cycle 3, we deployed a working version of the artefact for a period of two weeks and assessed its practical feasibility and participants' experiences of using it.

Through our research, we worked with job seekers recruited at two JR training sites, together with their trainers, to understand the barriers surrounding unemployment and job seeking in Cape Town, South Africa. From our findings, we discuss combinations of technologies and approaches that may support job searching, including mobile and web-based methods, door-to-door and electronic searches, and SMS and email communication. We identified participants' communication preferences as well as the barriers associated with using their preferred modes of communication. Finally, we deployed an application that combined some of the participants' existing approaches to communication and job finding with selected practices used by employment-search platforms.

Our two NGO partner organisations, Afrika Tikkun and The Zanokhanyo Network, provide job-readiness programmes for unemployed individuals seeking to improve their employability. Working with two organisations provided an opportunity to compare experiences across two organisational settings and to strengthen the contextual richness of the study. Our participants included job-seeking students as beneficiaries, their trainers as intermediaries, and the researcher as an observer, interviewer, data analyst and software developer. Participants contributed to the different stages of the Co-design process, while the researcher remained responsible for the technical implementation of the resulting artefact.

We identified what worked and what did not work for the participating low-skilled job seekers and proceeded to develop and deploy an application to assess its practical feasibility. Through the research, several methodological and design trade-offs emerged. These are discussed as insights for future work with unemployed and marginalised job seekers and for future co-design research in similar settings.

\section{Research Question}

{\setlength{\parindent}{0pt}
\setlength{\parskip}{1em}
\setlist[enumerate]{leftmargin=*, font=\bfseries}

In this research, we sought to understand how low-skilled job seekers use ICTs when searching for work, the barriers they experience when using these technologies, and how appropriate ICT-supported solutions might assist their job-seeking activities. Thus, we ask:

\begin{enumerate}[label=RQ\arabic*:]
    \item \textit{In what ways are the low-skilled using existing ICTs to find jobs?}
\end{enumerate}

Question one allows us to understand how existing ICTs fit into the lives and job-search practices of job seekers so as not to introduce off-the-shelf solutions without first considering whether those solutions are appropriate and usable for the participants.

\begin{enumerate}[label=RQ\arabic*:, resume]
    \item \textit{What are the barriers to the use of ICTs for job searching for the low-skilled?}
    \item \textit{Which technology or technologies are appropriate for low-skilled job seekers to find jobs?}
\end{enumerate}

Questions two and three help us to identify gaps and barriers associated with existing technologies and to explore whether new technologies, or combinations of existing technologies, may better support the job-search process. To investigate these questions, we interacted with low-skilled job seekers to understand their experiences, preferences and abilities through a range of research and co-design activities.

Then we ask:

\begin{enumerate}[label=RQ\arabic*:, resume]
    \item \textit{What are the salient design requirements for ICTs used to assist job seekers in job finding?}
\end{enumerate}

Through the various interactions, we were able to determine which issues arose more frequently, i.e.\ which were salient, and which requirements became particularly important during the co-design process. This contributes to understanding the design of technologies for low-skilled job seekers and other marginalised users who may experience similar technology-related and employment-related barriers.

Finally, we asked:

\begin{enumerate}[label=RQ\arabic*:, resume]
    \item \textit{What are the trade-offs to be made when performing co-design with low-skilled, unemployed or marginalised individuals from NGOs?}
\end{enumerate}

Because of the various methods used to interact with our participants, we were able to discuss the trade-offs, in other words, which techniques or approaches worked better under different circumstances and to what extent particular methods should or should not be applied. Thus, the study contributes to understanding co-design and NGO engagement in technology-supported employment initiatives within community-based settings.
}

Together, these questions shape the dissertation, which follows the path of the research itself, from the problem, through the people, to the design, and back to the questions. Chapter~2 sets the scene, introducing the two organisations, the problem of unemployment and the job readiness programmes. Chapter~3 then gathers the ideas, drawing together the literature that informed the study and the recent work that shows its issues remain relevant. Chapter~4 explains how the research was done, describing the \tme{} cycle, the participants, the ethical considerations and the approach to analysis. The next three chapters follow the three co-design cycles. Chapter~5 seeks to understand, presenting the barriers raised by job seekers and trainers in Cycle~1 and the directions these suggested. Chapter~6 tests an idea, showing how participants responded to a PowerPoint prototype in Cycle~2. Chapter~7 puts the idea to use, following the working artefact, Khanya Job Network, through a two week deployment in Cycle~3. Chapter~8 steps back to ask what the three cycles reveal about co-design, about ICT for job searching and about the limits of technology. Finally, Chapter~9 returns to where the dissertation began, answering the five research questions and setting out the contributions, limitations and future work.
