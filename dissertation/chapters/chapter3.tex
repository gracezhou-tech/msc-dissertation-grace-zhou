
\chapter{Literature Review}

This chapter discusses related work in unemployment and development.

\textbf{Since the empirical research for this dissertation was conducted in 2016–2017, this literature review combines the literature that framed the original study with more recent work. The more recent literature is not presented as literature that informed the original design decisions. Rather, it is used to determine whether the issues identified during the study remain relevant, to position the findings within subsequent ICT4D and Participatory Design research, and to provide concepts that are returned-to in the discussion chapters.}

\section{Development and Co-design}

Development is “the process of improving the quality of all human lives and capabilities by raising people’s levels of living, self-esteem, and freedom” [Todaro and Smith, 2015]. However, to improve the living standard/“people’s levels of living” [Todaro and Smith, 2015] there is a need for money and other resources. To earn money or acquire resources, one needs to be employed or own a business. Various forms of technology are increasingly seen as enablers of development [National Planning Commission, 2012]. However, development often advances with great imbalance, often termed the digital divide [Feldmann, 2003, Sambasivan et al., 2010, Nicola et al., 2011]. In South Africa, the digital divide has been very great, causing a major disparity between the rich and the poor. As with the digital divide, there is also a great disparity in the unemployment rate in South Africa across different groupings.

\textbf{More recent ICT4D literature cautions against understanding this divide only in terms of whether people have access to technology. Unwin [2017] argues that ICTs are not automatically developmental and that their effects must also be considered in relation to inequality, power and the interests of marginalised people. Heeks [2022] similarly describes “adverse digital incorporation”, where people may be included in a digital system but participate on terms that allow more advantaged actors to obtain disproportionate benefits. Thus, in this dissertation development is not taken to mean simply providing job seekers with another technology. The more important question is what job seekers are able to do through the technology, who benefits, and what inequalities remain after access is provided.}

The South African unemployment level stood at 25.2\% in 2014 by the strict definition of unemployment. However, there was a great disparity in unemployment levels when analysed along the groupings of race, age groups and skill levels. Zeroing into the unemployment level among the youth in South Africa (aged 15–24), the unemployment rate was higher, at 36.1\%, and further zeroing into black African youth in South Africa, the rate was even higher, at 39.4\% by 2014 [Statistics South Africa, 2015]. South Africa has the goal of reducing unemployment to 6\% by 2030 [National Planning Commission, 2012]. These statistics indicate the nation’s need to pay greater attention to unemployment issues among the youth and marginalised communities.

\textbf{These older statistics are retained because they show the labour-market conditions around the period in which this research was conceived. However, the problem has not since disappeared. In the second quarter of 2026, South Africa's official unemployment rate was 33.6\%, representing approximately 8.5 million unemployed people, while unemployment among youth aged 15–34 was 47.4\% [Statistics South Africa, 2026]. Statistics South Africa further reports that youth unemployment increased by 9.9 percentage points between Q2 2016 and Q2 2026. Thus, the 6\% National Development Plan target is even further from the present labour-market position, and the employment-access problem motivating this research remains very relevant.}

\textbf{At the same time, these figures reinforce the need to limit what can reasonably be claimed for an ICT intervention. An employment application cannot itself create sufficient labour demand or remove the wider economic causes of unemployment. Its contribution is potentially narrower, reducing some of the costs and difficulties involved in finding, interpreting and responding to available opportunities. This distinction between structural unemployment and barriers within the job-seeking process is returned to throughout the dissertation.}

In South Africa, the financial services, information technology and business services have grown employment, however, mainly for the skilled [National Planning Commission, 2012], thus a need for greater efforts in the inclusion of marginalised individuals. Technologies are generally considered unimportant in underdeveloped communities unless the technologies will contribute to the economic emancipation of the underdeveloped [Lorini and Chigona, 2014].

South Africa is faced by a shortage of skilled labour, which raises the income of the skilled and thus increases wage inequality in the country [National Planning Commission, 2012]. The field of Information Communication Technologies for Development (ICTD/ICT4D) has had several researchers investigating how to alleviate inequalities using ICTs: farming improvement projects to provide agricultural information to farmers [Gandhi et al., 2007], money transfer [Dyson et al., 2007], job finding information [Qiang and Kuek, 2012, Chepken et al., 2012a], business starter software for under-developed start-ups [Brittan, 2011], water service delivery [Ssozi-Mugarura et al., 2016] and many other infrastructural improvement and information access systems. The money transfer system M-PESA, which makes use of mobile technology to send money, is one example of a large-scale mobile intervention. These systems aim to reduce some inequalities between communities through the use of information and communication technologies.

ICT4D/ICTD is a discipline that focuses on ICT initiatives that contribute to social or economic development [Chepken et al., 2012b], often in regions of Africa, Asia and Latin America [Chepken et al., 2012b]. ICT4D projects have been critiqued for sometimes reflecting the political or institutional agendas of those in power, thus ICTD projects should transparently state action plans, beneficiaries and benefactors [Elkhatib et al., 2015]. South Africa has also identified the potential of ICTs in the National Development Plan [National Planning Commission, 2012]. ICT4D is understood in this dissertation as both an academic discipline and a field of practice in which information and communication technologies are used towards development [Chepken et al., 2012b]. In many past studies, ICT4D has been used to aid projects around health, education, agriculture and social communication [David et al., 2013, Chepken et al., 2012b].

\textbf{More recent South African research similarly treats ICT4D as contextual rather than simply technical. Abubakre and Mkansi [2022] identify emotional connectedness, user-centred technologies and symbiotic relationships as mechanisms through which South African technologists undertake ICT4D. This is relevant to the present research because the artefact was not developed independently of its setting: the job seekers, trainers, NGOs, researcher and available employment services together formed part of the environment within which the intervention was designed and used.}

The Co-design approach to developing technology helps in improving people’s wellbeing, reducing creation costs by engaging their own ideas, and improving the adoption of technology [Steen et al., 2011]. Co-design can be perceived as closely related to Participatory Design, with the involvement of stakeholders in the design process intended to ensure that the end-products respond to users’ needs and context [David et al., 2013]. Co-design has become integrated with developmental initiatives because of two intersecting ideas: firstly in design, a move toward user involvement for product improvements and secondly, in development, a move towards social embeddedness of projects and the importance of locals in technology appropriation [David et al., 2013]. Discussions around Co-design have five main aspects of consideration: stakeholders, context, ownership, social learning, and sustainability [David et al., 2013]. In other words, Co-design involves selecting relevant stakeholders, performing design within end-users’ contexts, encouraging ownership/adoption, obtaining mutual learning and considering whether ICTs can run on a long-term basis.

\textbf{However, participation should not be taken to mean that all participants automatically become equal designers. Researchers still influence the questions that are asked, the methods used, the available technologies and which ideas are eventually implemented. More recent South African work describes this in terms of “co-design readiness”. Till et al. [2025] identify emotional readiness, cultural readiness and technological readiness, and argue that readiness should be actively planned rather than assumed to develop by itself. This becomes useful when interpreting the different forms of participation that occurred across the three cycles in this research.}

\textbf{Iteration also places demands on participants. Dourish et al. [2020] show that repeated design engagement has practical and affective costs for participating communities. This is particularly important when the participants are unemployed job seekers whose time may also be required for job searching, temporary employment and family responsibilities. Thus, later chapters consider not only whether the project iterated, but also what the form and timing of participation allowed the job seekers to contribute at each stage.}

\section{Causes of Unemployment}

Gitau attributes the unemployment problem to a lack of skills among the low-income [Gitau, 2012]. In addition, minimum wage and work conditions restrictions from bargaining councils and wage boards, which are applied uniformly irrespective of company size, incur relatively higher labour costs on small businesses [Kingdon and Knight, 2001], which means that small businesses would not be motivated to employ many workers. This is true for only part of the unemployment problem, however, as barriers such as disempowerment and restrictions of the past apartheid period, lack of infrastructure, land and capital can also bar unemployed persons from employment. Poverty discourages job-search activities [Orkin, 1998] because searching for jobs incurs transport costs, CV creation costs and sometimes data costs.

\textbf{It is therefore useful to distinguish between the causes of unemployment and barriers encountered while searching for employment. Limited labour demand, economic inequality and discrimination are structural conditions that an ICT artefact cannot directly remove. Transport costs, CV preparation, repeated searches for vacancies, data costs and access to timely information are more directly connected to the process of finding work. The artefact developed in this study is mainly concerned with this second group of problems, rather than claiming to address unemployment in its entirety.}

Meissner and Blake attribute unemployment to weaknesses in career guidance support that young people receive from the school and family [Meissner and Blake, 2011]. In their work, Meissner and Blake made use of personas to show that low-income community youth face challenges of family pressure to find jobs as soon as possible, low or no guidance on how to propel their careers as well as a need to manage the highly-constrained resource, time. The young individuals had to juggle between school and watching over younger ones, or between working and watching over younger ones. They proposed to address this problem by offering career guidance which involved using a computer and website, but also admitted that these individuals were time-constrained and lacked advisors who would give them advice when using the websites.

Furthermore, research has shown that users with low education levels prefer to receive job notifications through their phones, via SMS or call as opposed to via emails [White et al., 2012]. This indicates that they may prefer simple mobile technology over computer-based or more advanced technologies. Individuals without college-level education tend to perform worse on computer-based solutions than those of college education level [Algarín, 2009]. However, email addresses are often requested or required on job-searching applications and websites like Gumtree, Indeed and Freelancer, thus these requirements can exclude some job seekers. The proposed intervention in this dissertation should therefore allow unemployed job seekers to make use of technology means that are accessible to them.

Moreover, Donner and Gitau identify an important category of technology users: mobile-only users [Donner and Gitau, 2009]. Mobile-only users do not have the luxury of doing tasks with the most optimised devices, for example PCs for typing and mobile phones for making voice calls, because they have mobiles as their main means of communicating and task completion. These individuals could be better designed for in a fashion that does not assume knowledge or ownership of desktop computers.

Meissner uncovered that users would like the best-paying jobs rather than the most fulfilling job. The findings also included that users do not get information about tertiary education and jobs frequently enough, and job availability information was sometimes outdated [Meissner and Blake, 2011], highlighting the demand for relevant information and the need to access information quickly [Bhavnani et al., 2008].

Since job seekers face many challenges including lack of skills, transport cost, CV creation cost, data cost, labour-market restrictions, the continuing effects of apartheid, lack of infrastructure, land and capital, poverty and poor career guidance, it is evident that unemployment is a multidimensional issue which requires various agencies to tackle it.

\textbf{Suoheimo et al. [2021] similarly examine marginalised youth unemployment in South Africa as a “wicked problem”: a problem made up of interconnected factors for which there is no single point of intervention and for which design solutions are necessarily partial. This framing is useful for the present research. Khanya Job Network is therefore understood as an intervention into particular job-search barriers rather than as a comprehensive technological solution to unemployment. This distinction is returned to when interpreting the intervention in Chapters 5–7.}

\section{ICT Interventions for Unemployment}

Several projects have attempted to use ICTs to tackle unemployment and poverty problems.

\textbf{The applications discussed in this section are not intended to represent an exhaustive list of employment technologies. They were selected because they demonstrate design issues relevant to the present study: South African or comparable African contexts, low-resource job seekers, low-bandwidth communication, job matching, alternative registration mechanisms and the role of human support. This provides a stronger basis for comparing the different interventions than simply comparing technologies from unrelated settings.}

Ummeli [Gitau, 2012] is a text-based mobile unemployment solution that was targeted at semi-literate individuals. We discuss this application because it was developed specifically for use in South Africa. Ummeli provides functionalities for its users to browse for jobs, post jobs and recommend jobs, create CVs and communicate with other job seekers. It was tested in the South African township of Khayelitsha [Gitau, 2012]. However, the application was registered for Vodacom cellular network users only. This meant that other unemployed candidates registered on other cellular networks such as MTN and Cell C would be left out.

\textbf{Ummeli is therefore treated here mainly as an important historical South African ICT4D example rather than as evidence of the present job-search environment. Of particular relevance is the design trade-off it exposes: the use of an accessible mobile channel can increase access for one group while a network-specific requirement simultaneously excludes another group.}

Babajob is a job searching network that connects job seekers to employers, particularly connecting poor job seekers with few skills to employers. Babajob utilises cell phone numbers to register job seekers as an alternative to email addresses; and involves people who may guide the use of technology by non-experts. Babajob’s interface is simple with few steps required from a job applicant as opposed to some conventional job-search platforms. Babajob also allows users to apply directly through the site, as well as subscribe to receive SMS [Babajob, 2016], however, the facilities were local to India.

Like Babajob, Giraffe, which is a job-searching platform based in South Africa, allows mobile numbers to be used to create an account. However, Giraffe had the shortcoming of requesting South African identity numbers, which barred non-South Africans from accessing the job opportunities available through the website.

Greataupair is another website for job finding, which filters job seekers by age and salary. The website posts jobs and job seekers, however, it is designed specifically for housekeeping jobs and requires an email to register an account. Like Babajob, the Kazi560 application in Kenya is an early example of an SMS-based job-matching application where job seekers could subscribe to SMS messages for a small fee per SMS [Qiang and Kuek, 2012].

\textbf{These early applications remain useful because they illustrate recurring design choices rather than because the particular services necessarily remain unchanged: whether registration requires an email address, identity document or only a mobile number; whether job seekers must repeatedly search; and whether relevant opportunities can instead be pushed to users through SMS or similar low-bandwidth channels. These are also design questions that arose during the development of Khanya Job Network.}

The Indian government announced the National Skills Development Policy, which aimed at creating 500 million skilled employees by 2022, amongst its other goals. In Karnataka, India, the labour department implemented a national network of employment centres across the country where candidates/potential job seekers undergo an assessment of their background and skill level and, based on the outcome of the assessment, the centre identifies appropriate job vacancies or suitable training for the candidate. The centres were also reachable by calling in to speak to an agent or utilising a government-managed internet portal associated with the centres. Although the approach is sound, the state was unable to maintain the level of assistance required to reach its employability goal and thus there was potential for technology solutions to improve the accessibility of employment services [White et al., 2012]. Similarly, the South African government implemented the National Youth Development Agency (NYDA) to assist South Africans under 35 years with skills training, business start-up guidance and venture capital [NYDA, 2015]. Its reach relative to the scale of youth unemployment has remained a persistent limitation [Statistics South Africa, 2015], and more recent oversight has raised concerns beyond reach alone: the Auditor-General identified R71 million in irregular, fruitless and wasteful expenditure following a lapse in the agency's previously clean audit record, and a parliamentary portfolio committee has raised concerns about the agency's accessibility for rural youth and persons with disabilities [Sunday Times, 2026, Portfolio Committee on Women, Youth and Persons with Disabilities, 2025]. This mirrors a broader pattern also evident in the Indian employment-centre model discussed above: state-run youth employment interventions in resource-constrained settings face persistent challenges not only of limited reach, but of institutional capacity and accountability — a consideration that informed the decision to work with NGO partners rather than pursue a centralised state-run model in this research.

Yet with the presence of several mobile interventions around unemployment, mobile Applications for Agricultural and Rural Development (m-ARDs) face challenges of finding funds to run. Several models of m-ARDs are therefore based on obtaining funding through SMS revenues which are low and often must depend on external support from policymakers and government aid. Particularly in developing regions, m-ARDs face challenges such as lack of local content, poor-quality interfaces, influential powers due to monetary support, and asymmetric access to information [Qiang and Kuek, 2012]. For example, employers may have persistent access to internet and web platforms to post vacancies whereas job seekers are not always well empowered to post their profiles in order to market their skills. Voice-based systems can be utilised when working with the textually illiterate, as has been successfully used in a rural setting in India to help individuals find employment [White et al., 2012].

\textbf{Recent South African evidence shows that some of these access problems persist even when an intervention deliberately removes data costs. Mangadi and Petersen [2024], studying a data-free South African job-search application, found that facilitating conditions and ease of use influenced acceptance and use. Connectivity, digital skills and the practical ability to use the employment service therefore remain relevant even where data itself is provided at no charge. This supports the need to understand digital job access as more than merely placing vacancies online.}

Some have proposed microtasks as employment [Mtsweni and Burge, 2014, Zyskowski et al., 2015]. Microtasks are small tasks often taken out of larger tasks and offer compensation to individuals from around the world [Mtsweni and Burge, 2014]. Examples of microwork include image location, item classification, translation and survey filling. Microtasks are offered on different microtask platforms accessible through smartphones and other internet-connected devices. Examples of microtask platforms include Amazon’s Mechanical Turk, InnoCentive [InnoCentive Inc., 2017], Ajira, Jana and Samasource. However, research in a township in South Africa has shown that community members mainly did not have computers and did not consider smartphones as a potential resource for income generation, thus limiting their access to microtasks through smartphones [Lorini and Chigona, 2014].

Microtask platforms offer job seekers non-traditional avenues to seek employment, often with payment known upfront. Despite the potential of microtasks to assist with unemployment, their accessibility is hampered because they are generally digital and require computers or smartphones and internet connectivity, which some low-skilled job seekers do not have [Gupta et al., 2012]. Mturk [Amazon Mechanical Turk Inc., 2018] and TaskRabbit [TaskRabbit, 2013] have also historically restricted participation in various ways. Samasource obtained tasks from developed regions and made them available in developing countries such as Kenya, South Africa and Uganda. Jana started as a small-scale microtask platform in Kenya and Rwanda [Eagle, 2009] and used technologies including SMS and USSD.

\textbf{More recent research from Namibia gives a useful Southern African comparison. Keskinen et al. [2022] found that digital microwork required more than the availability of a platform: participants also required digital and English-language skills, suitable devices, connectivity, email, appropriate financial infrastructure and knowledge of the available opportunities. They propose trained “tech mediators” to assist community members in accessing these digital services. This raises an important issue for this dissertation: the trainers and NGOs involved in the research may not simply be temporary substitutes for missing digital skills, but may themselves form part of a sustainable employment-support system.}

\section{Is Mobile for Development?}

In the period in which this research began, mobiles had become some of the most accessible devices for communication in constrained settings [Bhavnani et al., 2008, GSMA Intelligence, 2014], and as such technologies were increasingly built around the use of mobile devices for access to information and economic markets. Mobile subscriptions had overtaken fixed lines [Feldmann, 2003], and mobile devices had several advantages that made them desirable. Mobiles are wireless, portable, allow personal control and are capable of supporting discrete digital tasks which can be interlaced [Donner, 2015]. Mobile devices are also useful for mobile money transfer [Donner, 2015].

\textbf{These claims should now be read as part of the historical mobile context of the original study rather than presented as new developments in 2026. The continuing issue is not whether mobile phones are now common, but whether mobile ownership results in effective access. Recent South and Southern African research continues to show that connectivity, affordability, skills, appropriate devices and human support affect what people are actually able to accomplish through digital services [Mangadi and Petersen, 2024; Keskinen et al., 2022]. Thus, access to a phone does not necessarily mean that a person can comfortably prepare a CV, manage email, upload documents or repeatedly search large numbers of vacancies.}

A study that involved job seekers in Cape Town, Johannesburg, Nairobi and Windhoek indicated that although the job seekers were able to use messaging and call services, they often did not have enough airtime on their devices, with an average of ZAR one in Cape Town [Chepken et al., 2012a]. Smart mobile devices are also useful for accessing the internet [Donner and Walton, 2013], however, their usage does not override the need for computer-based internet access or Public Internet Access venues (PAVs) [Donner and Walton, 2013].

Wireless solutions have some advantages over their wired counterparts: they can be faster to set up and may cost less; however, the cost is underestimated when infrastructure such as towers and power systems is included [Feldmann, 2003].

Intermittent mobile data connection can be caused by device battery life, airtime balance, movement between places, changes in signal and congestion [Donner, 2015]. Finding a solution around all the possible causes of intermittent access would simply be infeasible, but being knowledgeable about them can motivate the design of solutions that are mostly offline, battery conserving and cost-effective, and that avoid unnecessarily data-intensive interactions.

\textbf{For this dissertation, the important principle is therefore not that “mobile is for development”, but that the communication technology should fit the resources and practices of the intended users. This also explains why SMS, email and web access were treated as complementary possibilities rather than assuming that one channel would be appropriate for every participant.}

\section{Including the Masses}

ICTs have great potential in disseminating information in a timely and cost-effective manner [Elijah and Ogunlade, 2006]. However, acknowledging and designing with the knowledge that communications are often braided [Densmore et al., 2013] over different channels is essential. The possibilities of channels in braided communication include SMS, email, voice calls, video calls and social media. It is therefore important to develop solutions that cross platforms or, where possible, are platform independent [Molapo and Densmore, 2015], because developing an ICT that is suited for only one communication channel or one specific device can result in the exclusion of people who use other channels and devices.

\textbf{The continuing relevance of braided communication can therefore be understood as an inclusion issue. A person who cannot use one channel should not necessarily be excluded from the service altogether. This is particularly important in employment services because job seekers may move between NGO computers, personal phones, email, SMS and assistance from trainers depending on the resources available at that particular time.}

The value that a user gains by being provided with new technologies depends on how well the technology fits into the user’s lifestyle or communicative ecology. In some instances new technologies may improve the lifestyle of users where the learning requirements remain manageable. This reinforces the need for simplicity as identified as a major HCI enabler and is reflected in Shneiderman’s 5th, 6th and 8th principles [Shneiderman, 2004].

\textbf{The principles referred to here concern preventing errors, allowing users to reverse actions easily, and reducing the amount of information users must retain in short-term memory. Their relevance to the present study is that a job-search system intended for users with different levels of digital experience should reduce avoidable complexity instead of requiring users to understand the internal organisation of the system before obtaining useful information.}

\section{Human Empowerment \& IT Sustainability}

Human empowerment is crucial for the sustainability of an intervention. Users may contribute to a system if they perceive intrinsic motivation such as having fun or helping people they feel affiliated with [Zimmerman et al., 2011]. In the Ummeli research, Gitau identified that one of the most pressing needs of participants was to locate and apply for jobs online; however, their going online was not exclusively for job finding but also for entertainment-related purposes such as finding music, crafting profiles, sending birthday invitations and befriending people [Gitau, 2012].

Co-design can induce a sense of ownership as well as sustainability in a community [David et al., 2013], which can result from engagement in participatory research. A sense of ownership of technology can be an empowerment realisation and sustainability reinforcement.

\textbf{However, co-design should not be assumed automatically to produce ownership or sustainability. As discussed in Section 3.1, the amount and nature of participants' influence can vary, and the researcher retains some authority over implementation. The later discussion therefore considers sustainability separately from participation: whether participants influenced the design is one question, while whether an organisation, community or technical team can continue supporting the artefact after the research is another.}

In some cases, a technology system may require maintenance, in part by an IT expert or intermediary, and thus each developer should ensure that other developers are able to maintain or build onto the system in the case that it is deemed valuable enough to be continually maintained. Heimerl et al. [2015] found that it is important to empower a community to solve its own problems with existing knowledge and infrastructure.

\textbf{Keskinen et al. [2022] provide a useful extension to this argument through the role of trained technology mediators. Sustainability may therefore involve neither complete technological independence nor permanent dependence on the original researcher. A local intermediary or partner organisation may instead provide the human layer needed to maintain access, knowledge and support. This interpretation is particularly relevant to the trainers and NGOs involved in this dissertation.}

Information dissemination is a crucial aspect of empowerment [Chepken et al., 2012a]. For example, an artefact that informs people of vacancies and teaches lessons useful for the work for which they would like to be employable can be useful. Surfing the Internet is one way in which people can access information almost anytime; however, the ability to surf the internet is inhibited by lack of know-how [Lorini and Chigona, 2014], limitations of mobile access and search-engine results and presentation format. In a study with individuals who were semi-skilled and preferably looking for skills training, 60\% of the individuals indicated that they did not find satisfactory information, as search engines did not offer sufficiently relevant or geographically appropriate information and gave an overwhelming number of results [Paihama and Suleman, 2016]. Obtaining ten results was overwhelming for the participants, whereas when the number was reduced to four, participants were better able to complete their tasks. Because of the shortcomings of search engines and users’ difficulties in searching efficiently, word of mouth or person-to-person communication can sometimes be more relevant for finding job opportunities and skills training services.

A system can be sustainable either through automated functions or through human maintenance. In the original technological context of this research, having a completely self-maintaining IT system was not considered realistic where information and software requirements changed unpredictably. Ensuring that users derive value from the maintenance of a system can motivate continued use and support. Schwartz et al. [2013] found that unintended uses of devices may motivate users to maintain devices, learn advanced tasks and possibly develop a sense of ownership. A sense of ownership can reinforce freedom and lead to empowerment and sustainability. However, when empowered individuals are not IT specialists, a system may need to be maintained by a specialised IT expert or organisation.

\textbf{The rapid diffusion of generative AI since late 2022 changes this position to some extent. Large Language Models can now assist with software-maintenance tasks including debugging, program repair and code review [Hou et al., 2024]. Thus, the choice is no longer simply between a system maintaining itself and a human developer performing all maintenance manually. AI-assisted human maintenance may reduce some of the technical burden of sustaining a deployed artefact. It does not, however, remove the need for human validation, hosting, current employment data, privacy management, organisational responsibility and decisions about what the application should do.}

Sustainability can also be approached by ensuring the design of an artefact is not only provided with useful functionalities but also enables users to understand those affordances [McGrenere and Ho, 2000], as opposed to having hidden affordances. Designs should be useful for new users, easy to maintain and documented in a way that they can be built onto.

\section{Artificial Intelligence, Employment and Job Matching}

\textbf{Artificial Intelligence adds a newer dimension to the relationship between ICT and unemployment that was not present in the same form when the original fieldwork was conducted. Although AI itself is much older, the widespread public diffusion of generative AI from late 2022 substantially increased ordinary users' access to AI-based tools. AI may automate some tasks previously completed by people while complementing other forms of human work. The ILO [2025] estimates that approximately one in four workers globally are in occupations with some exposure to generative AI, but argues that transformation of jobs is currently more likely than the complete replacement of most occupations.}

\textbf{The effect may nevertheless differ according to the type of employment. Giwa and Ngepah [2024] found a significant negative long-run relationship between AI investment and low-skilled employment in South Africa. Their separate research on skilled employment indicates that AI can also create or complement opportunities for workers with more advanced skills. Thus, employment created through AI cannot simply be assumed to replace employment lost through automation because the new opportunities may require different skills from the jobs or tasks being displaced.}

\textbf{AI can also affect the job-search process itself. A job seeker with the necessary knowledge may use AI to search and compare very large numbers of vacancies, determine which opportunities are more closely related to their skills and prepare applications faster. Employers can similarly process and compare hundreds or thousands of applications more efficiently. This creates the possibility of better employee-to-job matching from both directions, but the advantage may not be equally distributed.}

\textbf{Experienced candidates generally have more documented employment, recognised skills and previous outcomes from which an automated system can assess them. First-time or less-experienced job seekers may provide fewer such signals. Li et al. [2026] describe a related problem in algorithmic hiring as a balance between “exploitation”, where employers select from groups with established track records, and “exploration”, where candidates whose potential is less well known are also considered. Thus, AI-supported recruitment may produce an experience–data advantage, where people who have already entered employment become easier for automated systems to assess and recommend than those still trying to obtain their first opportunity.}

\textbf{This raises a further ICT4D issue. Greater automation can reduce search costs, but people who know how to use AI to represent their skills, interpret job adverts and tailor searches may benefit more than people who do not have this knowledge. Employers may similarly gain greater information and screening capacity than individual job seekers. Whether this leads mainly to better matching or reproduces existing inequalities is therefore an issue that is returned to in the discussion rather than assumed in advance.}

\section{Position of this Literature Review}

\textbf{Taken together, the literature suggests that unemployment and job seeking should not be treated as the same problem. Some barriers are directly ICT-addressable, such as repeated searching for vacancy information, notification of appropriate opportunities and some aspects of CV preparation. Other barriers, including language, digital skills, self-presentation and interpretation of opportunities, can be supported by ICT but may continue to require human assistance. Structural barriers such as limited labour demand, discrimination and wider economic inequality remain beyond the direct reach of the artefact.}

\textbf{The literature also shows that participation itself requires interpretation. Co-design does not mean that every participant performs every design activity or has equal technical ability. The relevant question for this dissertation is therefore how participant experiences and ideas influenced the problem framing, selection of design directions and changes made to the artefact, while also making the researcher's own synthesis and implementation role visible.}

\textbf{These two concerns provide the main position from which the subsequent chapters are read. The study examines how employment-related problems were identified with job seekers, how a subset of those problems became ICT design requirements, how those requirements changed through successive cycles, and what was learnt when the resulting artefact was used. Khanya Job Network is consequently treated as a partial intervention within the wider problem of unemployment, rather than as a technological solution to unemployment itself.}
