\chapter{Cycle 1: Understanding Barriers to Employment}
\label{sec:barriers-raised}

\begin{chapterintro}
This chapter seeks to understand how job seekers use ICT and what stands in the way of finding work. It reports the first co-design cycle, in which trainers and job seekers described their experiences. It sets out the methods, presents the findings on participant background, barriers and suggested responses, and discusses what the cycle taught about the method and about employment support. It ends by showing how these findings led to the direction and requirements carried into the second co-design cycle.
\end{chapterintro}


%------------------------------------------------
\section{Methods}

Cycle 1 focused on understanding the experiences of job seekers before selecting a technological intervention. The same general set of research activities was conducted at both partner organisations. At each site, we conducted two trainer interviews, two student job seeker interviews and one brainstorming workshop. Participants were informed about the purpose of the research and signed informed consent forms before taking part.

The trainer interviews provided an intermediary perspective on the challenges experienced by job seekers and on the support already provided through the Job Readiness programmes. The student interviews provided more detailed accounts from job seekers themselves. Workshop 1 was then used at both sites to discuss employment related challenges with a larger group and to generate possible responses.

In this dissertation, ``Workshop 1'' refers to the same Cycle 1 workshop activity replicated at both research sites. It does not refer to one workshop being Workshop 1 and the other being Workshop 2.


%------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Trainer Interviews}

Two trainers from each organisation were interviewed. The interviews were used to understand the trainers' experiences of working with unemployed job seekers, the types of support provided through the Job Readiness programmes, and the employment related challenges they observed among the students they trained.

The trainers were treated as intermediaries who could provide additional contextual information. Their responses supplemented rather than replaced the accounts provided directly by the job seekers.


%------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Student Job Seeker Interviews}

Two student job seekers from each organisation were interviewed. The interviews gathered information about participants' educational and employment backgrounds, previous work experience, skills, job searching practices, use of technology and the challenges they experienced when looking for work.

The interviews also explored participants' previous employment, reasons for leaving jobs, access to financial and technological resources, language use and the ways in which they usually obtained information about employment opportunities.


%------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Workshop 1 at the Two Research Sites}

Workshop 1 was conducted at both TZN and AT. The broad purpose of the workshop was the same at both sites: to identify challenges experienced during job searching, generate possible responses and explore ideas that could inform an ICT intervention.

At TZN, Workshop 1 involved five student job seekers, the researcher and a research assistant. The workshop consisted of the following activities:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Survey and study primer:} The researcher asked questions about the participants, their use of technology and the challenges they experienced when searching for employment. Issues such as language barriers and lack of work experience were raised.

    \item \textbf{Ideas brainstorming:} Participants discussed possible responses to the challenges that had been identified and could add to ideas suggested by other participants.

    \item \textbf{Paper prototyping:} Participants were given an opportunity to represent some of their proposed ideas using simple paper prototypes.

    \item \textbf{Ideas presentation and discussion:} Participants presented and discussed the ideas that had been generated.
\end{enumerate}

At AT, Workshop 1 was conducted with 33 student participants. The researcher had originally intended to work with a smaller group similar to the TZN workshop, but the trainer requested that the larger class group be included. The workshop retained the broad focus on identifying employment challenges and discussing possible solutions, but the activity was adapted to suit the larger group. Participants discussed ideas among themselves and presented the resulting issues and proposed responses to the wider group. Detailed individual prototyping was not emphasised in this session.

The findings from the interviews and both Workshop 1 sessions were subsequently considered together when identifying the main barriers and possible intervention directions.


%------------------------------------------------
\section{Findings}

The interviews and workshops identified a range of employment related challenges. Some were concerned directly with finding information and preparing job applications, while others related to qualifications, financial resources, language, work experience and wider employment conditions.


%------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Participant Background and Employment Context}

In Cycle 1, four job seekers were interviewed, two from each organisation. Their highest qualifications ranged from Grade 11 and matric to post school diplomas. Participants reported different skills and previous forms of employment, including driving, waiting tables and cooking.

All four interview participants had previously been employed and each had held several jobs. At the time of the interviews, three of the four were unemployed, while one was employed but continued to search for a different job.

Participants reported leaving previous employment for different reasons, including insufficient income, transport costs, difficult working conditions and moving to another employment opportunity.

Two participants had bank accounts while two did not. Three had children who depended on them. All four participants indicated that they used friends when looking for employment, while only one reported using a mobile phone to search for jobs.

Participants generally rated their own English reading, speaking and writing abilities relatively highly, although some also described difficulties communicating in English. They similarly rated themselves as employable while still recognising barriers to employment.

For example, Participant 4 explained that some employment sites required qualifications that the participant did not have:

\begin{quote}
``those sites they need people with qualification which are high when I don't have''
\end{quote}

Participant 1 described another difficulty, explaining that existing work could itself make searching for another opportunity difficult:

\begin{quote}
``as a security officer, sometimes I'm too busy with my job and I can't get information on time''
\end{quote}

These accounts showed that participants' difficulties were not limited to whether vacancies existed. Finding suitable information, having the required qualifications and having sufficient time and resources to search for work were also important.

%------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Barriers Raised by Participants}
The interviews and workshops produced a wide range of barriers to employment and job searching, listed in full in Appendix F (Table F.1) and discussed by theme below. Most of the barriers below emerged during group brainstorming at the two Workshop 1 sessions, where a brief discussion among participants typically preceded one participant raising a hand to state a point, which was then recorded, often with others indicating agreement by gesture. Cost-related barriers, language barriers, and barriers relating to confidence and self-representation also appeared independently in the Cycle 1 interviews (Section 5.1.2), and are reported in the plural accordingly.

Financial and resource barriers took several forms. The cost of printing or typing a CV came up in both the workshops and the interviews, suggesting a shared difficulty. A participant raised a general shortage of money to fall back on while searching; another pointed to simply not having the equipment a job search requires.

Information and matching barriers followed a similar pattern. A participant described the difficulty of finding out what work was actually available in the first place; another raised the disadvantage of lacking personal connections or references, which could close off opportunities depending on being known to someone already working.

Skills, experience and language barriers mixed both patterns. A participant raised a lack of formal qualifications, another a lack of previous work experience. Another described difficulty with the language demands of job seeking. This often meant they needed fluency in English or Afrikaans. This issue came up independently in the interviews as well as the workshops, as did a lack of confidence going into interviews and difficulty representing one's own skills to an employer.

Recruitment, discrimination and exploitation barriers were raised during the large AT workshop. A participant raised bribery within recruitment; another described hostility toward submitted applications, including a suspicion that CVs were simply discarded without being read. In this session, the issue of sexual favours being solicited in exchange for employment was also raised.

Labour-market barriers included a shortage of available jobs and work that was insecure or only offered short-term. Separately, in the individual interviews, two participants described losing previous jobs not because of difficulty searching for work, but because the job itself did not pay enough or cost too much to reach: one explained, "When I worked in Spar I earned better money," while the other said, "I lacked transportation money to my workplace."

Two further points were raised about participants' own behaviour rather than their circumstances: spending time on social media instead of searching, and simply losing the motivation to keep looking.

The same broad difficulties appeared across the interviews and workshops, although the different methods produced different levels of detail. The interviews provided more individual accounts, while the workshops allowed participants to identify common problems and discuss possible responses together.

A number of the barriers were outside the direct control of an ICT intervention. For example, an application could not itself create sufficient employment or remove exploitative recruitment practices. Other barriers, such as lack of information about vacancies, the cost of repeatedly preparing documents and difficulty finding relevant opportunities, suggested areas where ICT could provide some support.


%------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Participant Suggested Responses}
\label{sec:suggested-responses}

As with the barriers above, our participants also discussed possible responses to the problems they had identified. These were not limited to technological solutions. Participants proposed responses involving communities, employers, educational institutions and government as well as ways of improving access to information.

Participant-suggested responses are listed in full in Appendix G (Table G.1) and discussed by theme below.

Several suggestions concerned community-based action: a participant proposed becoming more involved in the community; another suggested taking on volunteer work; others raised relying less on others to find opportunities, meeting regularly to discuss prospects, and bringing in more experienced people to offer guidance.

A smaller number targeted government and policy: a participant called on government and communities to help create jobs; another suggested government provide skills development directly.

One suggestion targeted recruitment practice: that companies should make it possible to apply for jobs online.

Education and guidance suggestions included building stronger partnerships between colleges and companies, and, raised by a participant with particular conviction during the AT workshop, that secondary school students should be steered toward college rather than university.

The remaining suggestions concerned access and personal development: placing printed employment information where people would see it, and investing more in one's own skills. Many were willing to pursue further studies if they had the funding to do so.

At the TZN workshop, many of the ideas generated by participants centred on finding employment information through an application. A different idea was an application that could assist people to improve their English or Afrikaans where language was a barrier to employment.

Participants at TZN were also invited to represent some of their ideas through paper prototypes. One participant left before the prototyping activity was completed because of another obligation. Two of the remaining participants indicated that they were not comfortable producing prototypes and left the drawing activity largely to the other participants.

\section{Discussion}

Cycle 1 provided information about the employment barriers experienced by participants, but it also provided important lessons about how the co-design methods worked in the two research settings.


%------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Interpreting the Barriers and Suggested Responses}

The barriers reported in Section~5.2.2, \textit{Barriers Raised by Participants}, did not relate to technology in the same way. Some could reasonably be addressed through an ICT intervention, some could be supported by technology but not resolved by it, and some were wider conditions that an artefact could not change. This distinction is developed further in Section~8.3.1, \textit{Designable, Supportable and Structural Barriers}.

Lack of information about vacancies was one of the barriers that could be addressed more directly through ICT. Participants also identified financial and resource related barriers, including the costs of preparing CVs, travelling while searching for jobs, and accessing the resources needed to search and apply for work. An ICT intervention could therefore potentially reduce some of this repeated effort by making employment information and application support more accessible.

Other barriers, including qualifications, experience, language and confidence, could potentially be supported through technology but could not necessarily be resolved by providing an application alone. A system could help a participant organise employment information or structure a CV, but it could not supply a qualification or previous work experience that the participant did not possess. Language related self-presentation could similarly require human assistance in addition to technological support, as discussed further in Section~8.2.9, \textit{Human Support Remains Part of the System}.

Still other issues, including insufficient job availability, bribery, exploitative recruitment and insecure employment, were wider conditions that the proposed artefact could not remove. We do not consider recruitment, discrimination and exploitation barriers directly addressable through ICT. These are better understood as structural problems within recruitment practice than as informational barriers. Bribery in recruitment was raised during Cycle~1 and is reported in Section~5.2.2, \textit{Barriers Raised by Participants}. The ethical implications of another concern raised during the workshop, involving the solicitation of sexual favours in exchange for employment, are discussed in Section~4.3.4, \textit{Handling Sensitive Disclosures}.

We interpret the job loss and insecure employment accounts as evidence of a broader pattern of employment instability, which is distinct from difficulty in searching for a job. An employment support tool may help someone look for another opportunity after losing work, but it cannot prevent the loss itself. This issue is returned to in Section~8.2.11, \textit{Employment Quality Matters}, where employment quality and stability are considered separately from simply obtaining a job.

Two of the barriers concerned participants' own behaviour rather than only their external circumstances. These were time spent on social media instead of employment related activities, and a lack of motivation to continue searching for work. They are analytically significant because they show participants reflecting on their own behaviour, rather than describing unemployment purely as something that happened to them.

The interviews also showed the importance of social networks in job searching. All four interview participants reported using friends when looking for employment. Employment searching in this context was therefore not only an individual interaction with a digital system. Human relationships and trusted intermediaries were also part of how participants obtained information and support.

The responses suggested in Section~5.2.3, \textit{Suggested Responses}, also extended well beyond technology. A participant suggested volunteering as a way of gaining work experience, and another suggested further skills development. Other suggestions concerned the link between education and employment, including stronger relationships between colleges and companies and improved career guidance. A participant also proposed that employment information be shared within the community, and another called for greater support for job creation. The recommendation that school students consider college rather than university is a strong stance. We report it as a position held by participants in this setting and not as general advice, and this study did not set out to test it.

\subsection{Using Different Methods for Different Types of Information}

The combination of interviews and workshops was useful because the methods supported different forms of participation.

The interviews allowed individual participants to describe their own employment histories, challenges and preferences in greater depth. Workshops were useful for identifying issues shared across participants and for allowing one participant's contribution to stimulate ideas from others.

However, the paper prototyping activity showed that asking participants to express ideas through drawing or written representations did not work equally well for everyone. At TZN, some participants were comfortable developing paper representations while others preferred to contribute through discussion.

The lesson was therefore not that job seekers were unable to participate in design. Rather, different participants were more comfortable contributing in different ways. This informed the decision to use more concrete representations and one on one walkthroughs in Cycle 2.

The relevant question is not whether every participant performed every design activity, but whether the method created conditions in which their experiential knowledge could materially influence the design.


%------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Group Size and Participation}

The two instances of Workshop 1 also showed how group size affected the kind of information that could be gathered.

At TZN, the smaller group made it easier for the researcher to hear individual contributions, ask follow up questions and work more closely with participants during the paper prototyping activity.

At AT, the trainer requested that the workshop include the larger class group, resulting in 33 student participants. The larger group allowed a wider range of issues and suggestions to be raised. Participants could also discuss issues among themselves before presenting them to the wider group.

However, this arrangement also reduced the researcher's ability to hear and engage with every discussion taking place within the group. Some ideas were presented after participants had already discussed them within smaller clusters, and there was less opportunity to explore how each participant had reached a particular conclusion. Detailed prototyping was also more difficult with the larger group.

The comparison therefore showed a trade off between breadth and depth. A large group could surface many issues quickly, while a smaller group allowed closer discussion of individual experiences and design preferences.


%------------------------------------------------
\subsection{The Role of Trainers and Intermediaries}

The trainers played an important intermediary role. They helped the researcher understand the existing Job Readiness programmes, encouraged participation and provided contextual knowledge about the organisations and their students.

The AT workshop also showed that intermediary influence could substantially change the way a research activity was conducted. The trainer's request to include the larger class group was informed by knowledge of the organisation and a desire to include more students. At the same time, the resulting group size changed the conditions under which the planned co-design activity took place. This was reported in an early form in \cite{jegede2016codesign}, where the intermediary-led session at AT yielded more issues than the smaller session at TZN.

Co-design therefore requires flexibility, but that flexibility also needs limits. Participants and intermediaries should be able to influence the process, particularly where they bring knowledge that the researcher does not have. However, one co-designer should not be able to change the research arrangement so extensively that the participation of others is weakened.

The issue is therefore not whether participants should have power to change the process, but how that power is balanced. Researchers need to consider which decisions fall within a participant's area of experience, how a proposed change affects the research method, and whether the change reduces the ability of other co-designers to contribute.


%------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Researcher Synthesis and the \texorpdfstring{\tme}{Tell-Make-Enact} Process}

Cycle 1 can also be understood through the \tme{} framework used in this study.

During the Tell activities, participants described their experiences of employment, unemployment, job searching and technology use.

During the Make activities, participants discussed possible responses and, at TZN, produced paper representations of some of the ideas.

The Enact activity involved the researcher comparing the issues and ideas generated across both sites and deciding which possibilities could reasonably be developed within the research. The final intervention direction was therefore informed by participant contributions but required researcher synthesis. Participants did not collectively specify one final system and the researcher retained responsibility for deciding how different ideas could be combined into a feasible prototype.


%------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Implications for Employment Support Design}

The findings from Cycle~1 did not point to a single application that could resolve unemployment. The design direction needed to remain focused on the parts of the job searching process that the research could reasonably influence. Vacancy information, application preparation, communication and the representation of job seekers' skills provided plausible areas for intervention, and these informed the functions described in Section~6.1.3, \textit{Functions Represented in the Prototype}. Other barriers required human, organisational or structural responses, and treating unemployment itself as a problem that an ICT artefact could solve would have overstated what the research could achieve.

The candidate directions that followed from this reasoning are set out in Section~5.4.1, \textit{From Findings to Candidate ICT Directions}.

\subsection{Selecting a Direction for Cycle 2}

The researcher considered four main factors when deciding which direction to develop further: research resources, the available research period, the priorities identified by job seekers, and the interests and existing activities of the partner organisations.

\paragraph{Research resources}

The project had limited financial and technical resources. Proposed directions that required specialised equipment, large public displays or extensive external infrastructure were therefore less feasible within the study.

\paragraph{Available research period}

The selected intervention needed to be developed and evaluated within the available research period. The project also required time for participant recruitment, data generation, analysis and subsequent co-design activities.

\paragraph{Job seeker priorities}

Participants repeatedly expressed an immediate need to find work and earn an income. Lack of vacancy information, the cost of searching and difficulty preparing or presenting employment information were therefore more directly aligned with the problem the study could address than interventions requiring long periods of training.

\paragraph{Partner organisation context}

The partner organisations were already supporting job seekers through activities including CV preparation, computer training and interview preparation. The researcher's volunteer work at the sites also showed that preparing and editing CVs could require repeated human assistance. A system that supported job seekers in organising their employment information and generating application documents could reduce some of this repeated work without replacing the broader support provided by the organisations.


Based on these considerations, the Fund Finder was not selected because it would depend on continuously updated information from external funders. The Problem Assessment App was considered too broad because the problems identified during Cycle 1 varied substantially and many required human or institutional responses.

The public digital jobs notice board was not selected because it would require additional equipment and would provide the same information to multiple users rather than allowing information to be tailored to an individual.

The language learning idea addressed a problem identified during Cycle 1 but would require a longer period of learning and did not respond as directly to participants' immediate search for employment.

The mobile jobs notice board, talent registry and job searching application were more closely aligned with the information, search and self representation problems identified during the cycle. Rather than selecting one of these ideas exactly as originally proposed, the researcher synthesised aspects of them into the direction taken forward.


%------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Requirements Carried into Cycle 2}

The direction taken into Cycle 2 was therefore a web based employment support platform that could bring together several related functions.

The initial direction included the following requirements:

\begin{itemize}
    \item collect and organise information about a job seeker's personal details, qualifications, skills and previous employment;
    
    \item provide a profile through which the job seeker's skills and employment information could be represented;
    
    \item support the preparation of a CV using information provided by the job seeker;
    
    \item support the preparation of a cover letter;
    
    \item provide employment related information and job readiness tips;
    
    \item support the identification of relevant employment opportunities; and
    
    \item reduce some of the repeated effort involved in searching for vacancies and preparing application documents.
\end{itemize}

These requirements were not treated as final. They represented the researcher's synthesis of the Cycle 1 findings and provided a starting point for further co-design.

The researcher used these considerations to develop a more detailed, non functional PowerPoint prototype. Unlike the paper prototypes produced during Cycle 1, the PowerPoint prototype provided participants with a more concrete representation of the proposed registration process, profile and application functions. This prototype became the artefact reviewed and refined with participants in Cycle 2.
