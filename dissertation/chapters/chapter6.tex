\chapter{Cycle 2: Prototype Review and Refinement}

\begin{chapterintro}
In this chapter, we present the second research cycle, which focused on reviewing and refining the design direction developed from Cycle 1. Unlike Cycle~1, which began without a predetermined intervention, this cycle took the single direction selected at the end of Cycle~1 and examined it in depth. Its contribution is a set of design considerations specific to that intervention, together with an understanding of how participants could influence a design when given a more concrete representation to respond to. Participants took part in one on one walkthroughs of a PowerPoint prototype representing the proposed employment support platform. Section 6.1 describes the methods used in the walkthroughs and the prototype presented to participants. Section 6.2 presents the findings and changes suggested by participants. Section 6.3 discusses what was learnt from using the prototype and how participants influenced the design. Section 6.4 presents the considerations and requirements carried forward into the working artefact used in Cycle 3.
\end{chapterintro}


%================================================
\section{Methods}

Cycle 2 focused on obtaining participant feedback on a more detailed representation of the intervention direction selected at the end of Cycle 1. The researcher developed a PowerPoint prototype that brought together the requirements and ideas generated during Cycle 1. The prototype provided a more concrete representation of the proposed platform than the paper prototypes used during the first cycle, but it was not yet a functional application.

The purpose of the Cycle 2 walkthroughs was to allow participants to examine the proposed registration process, job seeker profile, document generation functions, job notification options and other proposed features before the working web artefact was developed.

For the PowerPoint prototype walkthroughs, the researcher worked with 14 participants in total, eight from Afrika Tikkun and six from The Zanokhanyo Network. The participants included current students, former students and trainers, as shown in Table~\ref{tab:powerpoint_participants}. Participants signed informed consent forms before taking part. Their inputs during the walkthroughs were recorded and later transcribed.

We conducted one on one walkthrough sessions in which the researcher presented the PowerPoint prototype and explained the proposed functions. Participants were then asked to comment on what they liked, disliked, found unclear or wanted changed.

The walkthroughs were iterative. After a participant suggested a change that was considered relevant to the developing design, the researcher could incorporate that change before meeting the next participant. Consequently, later participants sometimes viewed a slightly different version of the prototype from the one viewed by earlier participants.

The prototypes were also presented to trainers. Their contributions provided an intermediary perspective to supplement the experiences and preferences provided directly by the job seekers.

Participants were not given physical compensation for taking part in these walkthroughs.

\subsection{Functions Represented in the Prototype}

The PowerPoint prototype brought together several related functions.

\paragraph{Job Seeker Profile}

The proposed platform collected information about the job seeker, including personal and contact information, qualifications, skills and employment history. This information could be organised into a profile representing the person's employment background and abilities.

\paragraph{CV Generation}

Information supplied during registration was intended to be used to generate a CV for the job seeker. This responded to the Cycle 1 findings concerning the effort and cost involved in preparing and repeatedly submitting CVs.

\paragraph{Cover Letter Generation}

The same registration information could also be used to generate a cover letter. This was intended to provide the job seeker with a document that could support applications for advertised vacancies.

\paragraph{Job Matching and Notifications}

The platform was intended to identify vacancies that matched information in the job seeker's profile, including skills, qualifications and other relevant information. Relevant vacancies could then be sent to the job seeker by SMS, email, or both.

A complete implementation was intended eventually to automate the retrieval and matching of vacancies. At this stage, however, participants were evaluating the proposed function through the PowerPoint prototype rather than interacting with an automated matching system.

\paragraph{Employment and Business Tips}

The prototype also included a Tips section containing information about finding work, preparing a CV, preparing for interviews, starting a business and other employment related activities.

These functions were presented as a starting design rather than as fixed requirements. The purpose of the Cycle 2 walkthroughs was to allow participants to question and refine them before development of the working artefact.

\section{Findings}

Participant feedback affected both the information represented in the platform and the way some of the proposed functions were designed. The findings included issues concerning self representation, qualifications, communication channels, reputation, and the presentation and naming of the platform.


%================================================
\subsection{Representing the Job Seeker}

The PowerPoint prototype originally limited users to approximately ten words when describing themselves. Participants indicated that they wanted more flexibility.

One participant suggested allowing approximately a paragraph, while another wanted users to be able to write as much as they considered necessary. The feedback showed that a short fixed description could restrict the information that job seekers wanted to provide about themselves.

Participants also suggested different kinds of information that could potentially be represented in a job seeker profile. One participant suggested that information about a criminal record could be included where this was relevant to an employment opportunity.

This was treated as a participant suggestion rather than as a standard requirement for all job seeker profiles. It nevertheless showed that participants were considering the kinds of information that employers might use when assessing a potential worker.


%================================================
\subsection{Qualifications and Skills}

The walkthroughs identified a problem with the way qualifications were represented.

Participant 3 explained that some job seekers might consider matric to be their highest qualification even though they also held N4, N5 or N6 certificates because these qualifications were absent from an earlier version of the prototype.

The design therefore needed to allow a wider range of qualifications to be represented rather than assuming that educational attainment could be captured through only a small number of categories.

The feedback also reinforced the importance of representing skills separately from formal qualifications. Job seekers could possess practical skills or experience that were relevant to employment even where these were not represented through a formal certificate.

This was important for the proposed matching function because the relevance of a vacancy would depend on how accurately the job seeker's qualifications and skills were represented.

%================================================
\subsection{Communication Preferences}

Participants supported providing more than one method for receiving job notifications.

Participant 4 was pleased that the prototype provided an SMS option because not all job seekers regularly used email and not everyone had an email address.

Participant 5 similarly explained:

\begin{quote}
``sometimes people don't have data''
\end{quote}

An employment support platform that depended entirely on regular internet access or email could therefore exclude some of the intended users.

The Cycle 2 feedback supported retaining SMS and email as alternative notification channels. Participants could later select SMS, email, or both according to their preferences and access circumstances.


%================================================
\subsection{Ratings and Reputation}

The proposed home page included a rating of the job seeker based on previous work performance.

Three participants did not support the idea because they believed that it could disadvantage inexperienced job seekers. A person looking for a first employment opportunity would have no previous rating and could therefore appear less desirable than a person who had already accumulated ratings.

The proposed rating raised a design tension. Information about previous work performance could potentially provide employers with useful information, but relying on ratings could also disadvantage people who had not yet had an opportunity to establish a work history.

The rating feature was therefore not treated as a settled requirement for the working artefact.

The naming discussion showed that participants were concerned not only with whether the system worked technically but also with how the platform represented job seekers to others.

\section{Discussion}

Cycle 2 showed how participant contributions changed when they were given a more concrete artefact to respond to. Unlike the open paper prototyping activities in Cycle 1, the PowerPoint prototype presented participants with specific proposed fields, functions and interface decisions that they could accept, reject or modify.


%================================================
\subsection{Using a More Concrete Prototype}

The paper prototypes produced during Cycle 1 were useful for expressing broad needs and intervention ideas, but they contained limited detail about how a resulting system might work.

The PowerPoint prototype provided a more detailed representation of the proposed platform without requiring the working system to be developed first. Participants could see how registration might be organised, what information could be collected and which functions could be made available.

This enabled participants to identify issues that had not been apparent when discussing the intervention only at a conceptual level. For example, the absence of N4, N5 and N6 categories became visible because participants could see the actual qualification options presented in the prototype.

Likewise, the proposed rating mechanism could be questioned because participants could consider how it might affect job seekers with and without previous work histories.

The value of the PowerPoint prototype was therefore not simply that it contained more visual detail than the Cycle 1 paper prototypes. Its main value was that it made specific design decisions visible enough for participants to critique them.


%================================================
\subsection{Different Ways of Contributing to the Design}

The Cycle 2 walkthroughs showed that participants did not need to create software in order to influence its design.

Participants contributed through their knowledge of job searching, employment applications, technology access and their own qualifications and experience. They identified missing qualification categories, questioned ratings, influenced notification choices, changed the amount of information that could be provided in a profile and commented on how the system should present job seekers.

The relevant question is not whether every participant performed every design activity, but whether the method created conditions in which their experiential knowledge could materially influence the design.

Programming remained the responsibility of the researcher. The researcher also had to combine sometimes conflicting suggestions and decide how the resulting requirements could be represented technically.


%================================================
\subsection{Conflicting Participant Preferences}

Participant feedback did not always point towards one agreed design.

The proposed rating system provides one example. Some participants considered previous performance information useful for employers, while others were concerned that it would disadvantage job seekers who had not previously been given an employment opportunity.

Communication preferences provided another example. Some participants were comfortable with email and internet based communication, while others valued SMS because it required less continuous internet access.

Co design in this cycle therefore did not require all participants to make the same recommendation. Differences between participants exposed design tensions that the researcher needed to consider when moving towards implementation.


%================================================
\subsection{Participant Influence and Researcher Responsibility}

Participant feedback materially influenced the requirements carried forward from Cycle 2. However, participants did not collectively specify every aspect of the resulting web application.

The researcher remained responsible for synthesising participant contributions, assessing technical feasibility and implementing the resulting artefact. This was particularly important where participants expressed different preferences or where a proposed function required technical decisions that had not been discussed directly during the walkthroughs.

The progression from Cycle 1 to Cycle 2 can therefore be understood as movement between Tell, Make and Enact rather than as a process in which participants and the researcher performed identical roles.

The PowerPoint prototype represented a Make activity arising from the Cycle 1 findings. The Cycle 2 walkthroughs provided another Tell activity as participants responded to the proposed design. Their feedback then informed another Make and Enact activity in which the researcher refined the requirements and developed the working artefact for the next participant interaction.

\section{Considerations for Cycle 3}

Cycle 2 resulted in a more clearly defined set of requirements for the working artefact. These requirements did not represent a final or complete system. They represented the functions and design decisions that had developed sufficiently to be implemented and evaluated with participants in Cycle 3.


%================================================
\subsection{Requirements for the Working Artefact}

The following requirements were carried forward:

\begin{itemize}

    \item \textbf{Registration and profile creation:}
    The artefact needed to collect personal information, contact details, qualifications, skills and employment history and organise this information into a job seeker profile.

    \item \textbf{Flexible representation of qualifications and skills:}
    The registration process needed to accommodate qualifications such as N4, N5 and N6 and allow job seekers to represent skills that were not necessarily captured by their highest formal qualification.

    \item \textbf{CV generation:}
    Information supplied during registration would be used to generate a CV that the job seeker could view, download and use when applying for employment.

    \item \textbf{Cover letter generation:}
    Information supplied by the job seeker would also be used to generate a cover letter that could accompany applications.

    \item \textbf{Job seeker self representation:}
    The design needed to provide sufficient space for participants to describe their experience and qualities rather than imposing the short description limit used in the earlier prototype.

    \item \textbf{Employment and business information:}
    The Tips section would provide employment related guidance, including information about finding jobs, preparing a CV, preparing for interviews and starting a business.

    \item \textbf{Vacancy matching:}
    Job opportunities would be selected according to information contained in the job seeker's profile, including skills, qualifications and other relevant profile information.

    \item \textbf{Multiple notification channels:}
    Job seekers would be able to select SMS, email, or both as methods for receiving vacancy information.

\end{itemize}

The proposed employer facing component remained part of the broader platform concept because making job seeker profiles visible to potential employers could provide another route through which employment opportunities might be created. However, employers were not participants in this study and the employer side of the platform was therefore not evaluated during the subsequent deployment.

The rating feature also remained unresolved because participant feedback showed both a possible benefit and a possible disadvantage. It was therefore not treated as a settled requirement for the working artefact.


%================================================
\subsection{Transition to Cycle 3}

Following the Cycle 2 walkthroughs, the researcher used the refined requirements to develop the working web artefact, Khanya Job Network.

The development of the web artefact took place between the Cycle 2 and Cycle 3 participant interactions. Under the cycle structure used in this dissertation, the artefact is therefore treated as an outcome of the Cycle 2 design process and as the artefact introduced and evaluated in Cycle 3.

Cycle 3 begins by describing the working artefact and the functionality that had been implemented. Participants then interacted with the artefact directly before it was deployed with them for two weeks. The next chapter therefore examines what occurred when the proposed functions moved from a PowerPoint representation into an artefact that participants could use in practice.
