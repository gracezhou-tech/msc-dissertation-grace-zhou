\chapter{Cycle 3: Working Artefact and Deployment}

\begin{chapterintro}
This chapter puts the idea to use. It describes the working artefact prepared for the third co-design cycle, the participants, and the two week deployment. It reports how participants used the artefact and how they represented themselves through it. It then discusses what these findings mean for co-design and for employment support, and presents the considerations arising from Cycle~3 that are taken forward into the broader discussion in Chapter~8.
\end{chapterintro}


%================================================
\section{Methods}

Following the Cycle 2 PowerPoint walkthroughs, the researcher developed the working web artefact, Khanya Job Network. The development took place between the Cycle 2 and Cycle 3 participant interactions and incorporated the requirements refined during Cycle 2.

Cycle 3 began with participants interacting directly with this working artefact. The same cycle then continued into a two week deployment in which participants received job notifications through SMS, email, or both. At the end of the deployment, evaluation interviews were conducted to obtain feedback on participants' experience of the artefact as a whole.


%================================================
\subsection{Working Artefact Prepared for Cycle 3}

The working artefact implemented the main job seeker functions developed through the previous cycles. These included registration and profile creation, CV and cover letter generation, employment and business information through the Tips pages, and the ability to select SMS, email, or both as communication channels for job notifications.

The broader design also included vacancy retrieval and matching. The intended complete system would automatically retrieve vacancies and compare them with information contained in the job seeker's profile. However, this component was not automated during the study. The manner in which the intended matching process was simulated during the deployment is described in Section~7.1.4.


%------------------------------------------------
\subsubsection{Technologies}

The working artefact was developed as a web application. Bootstrap version 3.3.7 and jQuery version 3.2.0 were used for interface responsiveness and presentation on the HTML and CSS interface. Participant information was stored in a MySQL database. PHP version 7.1.1 was used for communication between the web interface, server and database, while Apache provided the web server. The server and database were integrated using the XAMPP development environment.

Indeed was selected as the main source from which vacancies could be identified during the deployment because it provided a broad range of vacancies and sufficient information for the researcher to identify opportunities relevant to the participants.

The intended complete application would automate the retrieval and matching of vacancies from an online job source. During the research deployment, however, this retrieval and matching process was performed manually by the researcher.

At the beginning of the interaction, the researcher introduced herself and explained the purpose of the study. Each participant signed an informed voluntary consent form.

Participants were asked to perform a set of tasks using the working artefact. They were not first trained on how to complete the tasks so that the researcher could observe whether and where assistance was required.

The tasks included:

\begin{itemize}
    \item registering on the application;
    \item logging in;
    \item viewing the Tips section;
    \item viewing the generated CV;
    \item downloading the generated CV;
    \item viewing the generated cover letter; and
    \item downloading the generated cover letter.
\end{itemize}

Task completion was recorded using a task sheet on which completed activities were marked.

The iterative development process continued during these initial interactions. After the first two participants registered on the application, the researcher identified a problem affecting the storage of registration information in the database. The problem was corrected before continuing with the third participant.

The working artefact was also shown to three trainers, two from AT and one from TZN, to obtain supplementary feedback from the intermediaries. The trainers were not part of the ten job seekers taking part in the two week deployment.

Participants were not physically compensated for the initial walkthrough sessions.

\subsection{Two Week Deployment}

After registration and the initial interaction with the working artefact, the ten participants entered a two week deployment from late August 2017 to mid September 2017.

During the deployment, participants could make use of the CV and cover letter generated from their registration information. These documents were also emailed to them so that they could use them when responding to advertised vacancies. Participants could print the documents where a physical application was required.

Participants also received job vacancy notifications by SMS, email, or both, depending on the communication method they selected during registration.


%------------------------------------------------
\subsubsection{Vacancy Retrieval and Matching}

The complete design of Khanya Job Network was intended to retrieve vacancies automatically and match them to information stored in each job seeker's profile. This component was not automated during the deployment.

Instead, the researcher manually simulated the process that an automated vacancy retrieval and matching component was intended to perform. This was a Wizard of Oz style implementation of this particular function.

For each participant, the researcher:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item searched Indeed for available vacancies;
    \item considered the participant's stored profile information, including skills, highest qualification and other relevant information;
    \item selected vacancies that corresponded with the participant's profile;
    \item checked that the vacancy was still available; and
    \item sent the relevant vacancy information to the participant through SMS, email, or both, depending on the communication option selected during registration.
\end{enumerate}

The manual process applied specifically to vacancy retrieval and matching. Registration, profile creation, CV generation, cover letter generation, the Tips pages and the communication mechanisms used to deliver notifications were implemented functions of the artefact.

The manual matching process was not visible to participants. From their perspective, vacancies were received through the SMS or email communication channels intended for the complete system. This allowed participants to experience and evaluate the proposed job matching and notification service without claiming that the automated retrieval and matching component had been implemented.


%------------------------------------------------
\subsubsection{Job Notifications}

SMSPortal was used to deliver the SMS notifications.

A group called ``Job seekers'' was created for the participating job seekers. A template was used to keep the SMS messages concise and consistent.

Because an SMS could contain only a limited amount of text, the messages focused on three main types of information:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Job:} the job title or short description;
    \item \textbf{Job Location:} the location of the vacancy; and
    \item \textbf{Contact or application information:} information needed to follow up on the vacancy.
\end{itemize}

Email could provide more detailed information about a vacancy, while SMS provided a shorter notification that did not require the participant to be continuously online.


%------------------------------------------------
At the end of the two week deployment, evaluation interviews were conducted to understand participants' experience of the artefact as a whole. The interviews therefore covered the implemented functions used during registration and the initial walkthrough as well as participants' experience of receiving matched vacancy notifications during the deployment.

Some questions from the previous cycle were retained to allow comparison across cycles. Additional questions were introduced where new issues had emerged from interacting with the working artefact and using it over the two week period.

A standardised usability questionnaire was not used because the researcher wanted participants to be able to explain their experiences and wanted the flexibility to ask follow up questions where clarification was required. This was also useful where participants had different levels of English proficiency.

The interview instrument in Appendix C covered participant background, experience of the Job Readiness programmes, the utility and usability of the artefact, and participants' experience of SMS and email notifications.

Nine of the ten participants were available for the final evaluation. Eight participated in one-on-one face to face interviews. The ninth was interviewed by telephone because he was at his new workplace.

One participant had therefore obtained employment during the deployment period. However, the two week deployment does not provide sufficient evidence to attribute that employment outcome to the artefact.

\section{Findings}

The Cycle 3 findings combine evidence from participants' direct interaction with the working artefact, their use of the artefact and job notification service during the two week deployment, and the final evaluation interviews.


%================================================
\subsection{Initial Use of the Working Artefact}

All ten participants completed the initial application tasks. One participant experienced difficulty logging in because he had forgotten the password he had registered shortly before.

Participants who were actively looking for employment generally responded positively to the artefact and the proposed notification service. Participants were particularly interested in receiving job opportunities through SMS as well as email.

The Tips section also attracted participants' attention. One participant commented that he had not previously seen a job finding application that also provided this kind of employment guidance.

The initial walkthroughs also highlighted the importance of managing expectations. One participant was eager for the artefact to remain available for continued use after the study. The researcher explained that it was being evaluated as part of the research and would not be permanently deployed within the scope of the study. The expectation arose again despite the explanation, showing that the research scope sometimes needed to be repeated rather than explained only once.

One participant also experienced difficulty entering information because of limited English proficiency. This was important because information entered during registration was subsequently used to construct the participant's profile and generated employment documents.

The supplementary walkthroughs with the three trainers produced generally positive feedback. One trainer, however, did not want to receive frequent email notifications. This reinforced the value of allowing users to choose whether to receive notifications by SMS, email, or both.

\subsection{The Cost of Seeking Jobs}

The final evaluation reinforced the financial and practical costs associated with searching for employment.

When Participant 8 compared Khanya Job Network with searching for employment in person, the participant stated:

\begin{quote}
``There is another way like moving door-to-door... that one is very expensive''
\end{quote}

The participant later explained that with Khanya Job Network:

\begin{quote}
``you can approach anyone''
\end{quote}

Participant 6 similarly stated:

\begin{quote}
``It saves me from moving around. I don't have to go to dogs''
\end{quote}

Participant 8 noted that some employers did not want job seekers to arrive without an appointment:

\begin{quote}
``Other people they don't want a person to come in person because they need an appointment''
\end{quote}

Participant 6 also described the repeated effort associated with physical applications:

\begin{quote}
``not like going to write 20 CVs and sending it''
\end{quote}

Mobile data was another cost. When asked why he did not use his phone frequently when looking for employment, Participant 1 stated:

\begin{quote}
``Because the connection costs some bucks''
\end{quote}

These responses showed that the costs of job searching included transport, data, preparation of application documents and repeated physical submission of CVs.


%================================================
\subsection{Generated CVs and Cover Letters}

Participants responded positively to the documents generated by the artefact.

Participants 3, 6 and 9 particularly appreciated the CV format shown in Figure~\ref{fig:generated_cv}. Participant 9 stated:

\begin{quote}
``I appreciate the format of the CV, I just found out that that format is what employers are looking for''
\end{quote}

Participant 5 found the cover letter shown in Figure~\ref{fig:generated_coverletter} useful because it provided a shorter introduction to the applicant:

\begin{quote}
``cover letter it have [has] sort of details or introduction about myself. It's easy to read than a cv that have [has] a lot of details''
\end{quote}

The responses suggest that participants valued the ability to turn information supplied during registration into reusable application documents.


%================================================
\subsection{Custom Job Notifications}

Most participants responded positively to receiving daily vacancy notifications.

Participant 4 stated:

\begin{quote}
``emailing and SMSing were the best [features] for me. Since I am that kind of person who is always on the internet, it is very easy for me to know what is coming''
\end{quote}

Participant 3 valued receiving available jobs without having to perform the search independently:

\begin{quote}
``they give us the available jobs. Instead of going and search for yourselves''
\end{quote}

The same participant compared Khanya Job Network with another job alert service:

\begin{quote}
``they are different because Khanyo [Khanya Job Network] send you an email but Job alert give you a late job post... some of the job from job alert are the post that I don't qualify for''
\end{quote}

This response highlighted the importance of relevance rather than simply sending a large number of vacancies.

These findings relate to participants' experience of receiving vacancies that had been selected according to their profiles. Because the vacancy retrieval and matching process was manually simulated during the deployment, the findings do not evaluate the technical performance of an automated scraper or matching algorithm.


%================================================
\subsection{Accessing the Internet}

All participants reported using the internet in some form. Most used mobile phones, one also used a tablet, and participants could make use of computers at the NGO sites.

Participant 7 did not own an internet enabled device but explained:

\begin{quote}
``I have access when I get here or when I go to Internet Café''
\end{quote}

The findings therefore distinguished between using the internet and having continuous personal access to an internet enabled device and sufficient data.

Providing both SMS and email allowed the notification service to accommodate some of these differences in access.


%================================================
\subsection{Self Representation}

The registration process asked job seekers to describe their strengths and qualities so that this information could contribute to their profile and employment documents.

Participants' descriptions varied substantially in length, language and level of detail.

Some participants provided very short descriptions. Participant 3 wrote:

\begin{quote}
``I m motivated and hard worker''
\end{quote}

Participant 6 wrote:

\begin{quote}
``I am a hard-worker. I am willing to work.''
\end{quote}

Other participants provided considerably more information. Participant 8 described being a hard worker, enjoying working with people, wanting to gain more experience and having completed matric. Participant 9 described personal qualities, English proficiency, work permit status and previous teaching experience.

Some descriptions contained spelling or grammatical errors. Participant 1 wrote:

\begin{quote}
``im every dedicated person who can get long with poelple''
\end{quote}

Participant 10 described himself as:

\begin{quote}
``I am time conscious, I a team player, I am a quick leaner.''
\end{quote}

The researcher subsequently made spelling and grammatical corrections to some of these descriptions after data collection. These edits were made by the researcher and were not automatically generated by the artefact.

The descriptions showed that providing a free text field allowed job seekers to describe themselves in their own words, but did not necessarily result in text that represented them as clearly as they intended.

\section{Discussion}

Cycle 3 moved the research from participants commenting on representations of a possible platform to participants interacting with a functioning web artefact and using its services over two weeks. This produced further methodological lessons about participation as well as design implications concerning job seeker representation, vacancy matching, communication access and human support.


%================================================
\subsection{Implications for Co Design}

The three research cycles involved different forms of participant contribution. Cycle 1 focused on employment experiences, barriers and possible intervention ideas. Cycle 2 allowed participants to critique and refine a more concrete PowerPoint representation. Cycle 3 allowed participants to interact with implemented functions and evaluate their experience during deployment.

The short duration of the Job Readiness programmes limited continuity across these cycles. The same participants could not always remain involved from the beginning of the study through to the final deployment. Consequently, later participants often encountered an artefact that had already been influenced by earlier groups.

This does not mean that participant influence depended on participants performing the technical development themselves. Participants influenced the system by identifying problems, proposing functions, rejecting or modifying design ideas, commenting on prototypes and evaluating the working artefact.

The relevant question is not whether every participant performed every design activity, but whether the method created conditions in which their experiential knowledge could materially influence the design.

The researcher nevertheless retained responsibility for synthesis and implementation. The researcher decided how conflicting suggestions were handled, which requirements could be implemented within the study, and how participant contributions were translated into software.

Cycle 3 also reinforced the importance of expectation management. Some participants wanted the artefact to remain available after the research despite explanations about the study's limited scope. Where participants changed across cycles, expectations had to be explained to each new group rather than being negotiated only once at the beginning of the project.

Recruitment for the working artefact walkthroughs was also affected by participants' other responsibilities. Potential participants had job searching activities, part time work, childcare and other commitments. This showed that time given to participation was itself a resource. Compensation may therefore affect whether people are able or willing to make time for research, but the study does not establish that compensation would necessarily produce more accurate or more favourable participant responses.


%================================================
\subsection{Skills, Qualifications and Self Representation}

The Cycle 3 findings showed that employment support systems need to represent both formal qualifications and the wider skills and experience that job seekers possess.

Five of the nine participants who completed the final evaluation had matric or lower as their highest formal qualification. However, participants also reported practical skills and previous forms of employment that could not be represented adequately through highest qualification alone.

The qualification concerns raised during Cycle 2 and the self descriptions collected during Cycle 3 both demonstrate that the information structure of a job seeker profile matters. A restricted set of categories can omit relevant qualifications, while an unrestricted free text field can create a different problem where a participant has difficulty expressing experience in written English.

The design challenge is therefore not simply to collect more information. Information must be structured sufficiently to support matching and document generation while still allowing job seekers to represent skills and experience that do not fit neatly into predefined categories.

The self description findings also show a possible role for assistance. Some participants could benefit from support with spelling, grammar or structuring information. However, such support should not remove the job seeker's control over how they are represented.


%================================================
\subsection{Vacancy Matching and Employer Feedback}

Participants valued receiving vacancies that had already been selected according to their circumstances. Participant 3's comparison with another job alert service showed that receiving many vacancies was less useful when the vacancies did not correspond with the person's qualifications.

The Cycle 3 deployment therefore provides evidence that participants valued the service concept of receiving filtered vacancies. It does not demonstrate the technical effectiveness of an automated matching system because the retrieval and matching process was performed manually by the researcher.

A future implementation would need to automate this process while preserving the relevance experienced during the manually simulated service. This would require accurate job seeker profiles, current vacancy information and appropriate criteria for determining whether a vacancy is relevant to a particular person.

The findings also identified a problem beyond vacancy discovery. Participant 2 described submitting an application and then not knowing what had happened:

\begin{quote}
``I have tried but I don't know what is happening. Maybe it's me who have tried in a difficult way because when SMS have entered to my email and I click there, and they tell me to submit my CV. I have tried I'm still waiting.''
\end{quote}

This indicates that finding a suitable vacancy and submitting an application do not complete the employment search process. Feedback from employers is another part of the experience.

One participant obtained employment during the deployment period and reported that the work he eventually performed differed from the role initially described to him. The employer was not part of the study, so the research cannot establish why this change occurred.

This limitation points to a wider stakeholder issue. The artefact developed from a job seeker centred perspective, but once the design included job seeker profiles, vacancy matching and possible employer access, employers became consequential stakeholders. Their requirements, trust concerns, information needs, search practices and reasons for using such a platform were not evaluated in this study.


%================================================
\subsection{Costs, Access and Communication Channels}

The Cycle 3 findings reinforced barriers that had already appeared in Cycle 1.

Participants described transport costs associated with moving between potential employers, the effort involved in preparing and submitting multiple CVs, and the cost of mobile data used to search for employment.

The artefact could reduce some of these repeated activities. Generated documents could be reused, while selected vacancies could be delivered directly to participants rather than requiring them to repeatedly search several sources.

However, this does not mean that the artefact removed the wider costs of seeking employment. Participants could still require data, transport or other resources when following up on an application, attending an interview or beginning a new job.

Participants also had different forms of technology access. Some owned smartphones and had regular internet access, while others depended partly on NGO computers or internet cafés.

For this reason, SMS and email served different purposes. SMS could provide concise vacancy information without requiring a participant to remain continuously online, while email could provide more detailed information.

The findings therefore support allowing more than one communication channel where users differ in device ownership, data access and communication practices.


%================================================
\subsection{Human Support and Intermediaries}

The deployment did not remove the role of the NGOs and trainers in participants' employment search.

The NGOs provided computer access, job readiness training, CV support, interview preparation and other forms of assistance. Trainers also played an intermediary role during the research by introducing activities and encouraging participation.

Participant 10 reported that he continued to visit TZN during his days off even though he had access to both a smartphone and a laptop. The study did not establish why he continued to use the NGO site, but the observation shows that personal access to technology did not make the organisational setting irrelevant.

The self representation findings provide another example. A system could collect profile information and automatically generate documents, but some participants could still benefit from human assistance when describing their skills or checking how they were represented.

The deployment itself also exposes a further human role that would otherwise be easy to overlook. The relevant vacancies received by participants depended on manual searching and matching performed by the researcher. Participants experienced the service as a technology mediated notification service, but the matching process depended on human labour during the study.

A future version would therefore need either a reliable automated vacancy retrieval and matching component or an organisational arrangement capable of sustaining the human effort needed to perform this work. The two week deployment does not establish the longer term sustainability of either approach.


%================================================
\section{Considerations Arising from Cycle 3}

Cycle 3 demonstrated that participants could use the implemented registration, profile, CV, cover letter and information functions and could evaluate the experience of receiving vacancies through SMS and email. It also identified several issues that cannot be resolved from the deployment alone.

First, the study evaluated the experience of receiving manually matched vacancies rather than the technical performance of an automated vacancy retrieval and matching component. A future implementation would therefore need to develop and evaluate that component independently.

Second, the study focused primarily on job seekers and intermediaries. The employer side of the proposed platform was not evaluated even though employers would become important stakeholders in a system involving job seeker visibility, vacancy matching, recruitment feedback or reputation.

Third, the two week deployment was suitable for gathering participant feedback on the artefact and notification service but was too short to establish longer term employment outcomes or system sustainability. The participant who obtained employment during this period cannot be treated as evidence that the artefact caused the employment outcome.

Fourth, the deployment showed that technology did not remove the need for human and organisational support. Some job searching activities could be supported directly by the artefact, while other activities continued to depend on interpretation, assistance, access to organisational resources and interactions with employers.

These considerations are taken forward into Chapter 8, where the findings from all three cycles are examined together in relation to the possibilities and limitations of co design and technology for employment support.
