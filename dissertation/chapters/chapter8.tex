\chapter{Discussion}

\begin{chapterintro}
This chapter brings together the findings from the three research cycles and considers what they reveal about co-design with job-seekers, the design of employment-support technologies, and the limits of technology as a response to unemployment-related challenges. The purpose is not to repeat the findings presented in Chapters 5--7, but to interpret what they mean when considered across the study. Three main arguments are developed. First, co-design is not made meaningful simply by inviting participants into interviews, workshops or prototype activities. Its effectiveness depends on the conditions created for participation, including readiness, group size, continuity, stakeholder composition and the distribution of decision-making power. Second, designing for job-seekers requires attention to more than access to vacancies. Job-seekers also need relevant opportunities, ways to represent their skills and qualifications, affordable means of searching and communicating, application support and, in some circumstances, trusted human assistance. Third, not every barrier identified through co-design should become a technology requirement. Some barriers can be reduced through an artefact, some can only be supported in part, while others arise from wider conditions that the artefact cannot directly change. The discussion therefore considers both the possibilities and the boundaries of the methodology and of the resulting technological intervention.
\end{chapterintro}


%================================================
\section{The Limits of Co-design: Implications for Co-design Methodology}

A central methodological lesson from this study is that co-design is conditional. Participation cannot be assessed simply by counting the number of workshops conducted, the number of people present, or whether participants produced prototypes. What is more important is whether participants were able to influence how the problem was understood and how the resulting intervention developed. Across the three cycles, participants contributed in different ways. Job-seekers described their experiences of employment and unemployment, identified barriers to finding work, suggested possible responses, represented some ideas through paper prototypes, walked through more concrete representations, challenged design assumptions and later interacted with a working artefact. Trainers contributed another form of knowledge based on their continued experience of supporting job-seekers, while the researcher remained responsible for facilitating the research, synthesising contributions and developing the technical artefacts. The relevant question is not whether every participant performed every design activity, but whether the method created conditions in which their experiential knowledge could materially influence the design.


%------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Tell--Make--Enact Across the Research Cycles}

The Tell--Make--Enact activities used in the study did not correspond neatly to the boundaries of the three numbered research cycles. Instead, they extended across the cycles as an iterative process. This interpretation is consistent with the broader view of co-design as involving different forms of telling, making and experiencing possible futures rather than requiring every participant to perform an identical design role \cite{sanders2008,sanders2014}.

Cycle 1 contained substantial \textit{Tell}. Through interviews and workshops, job-seekers and trainers described experiences of employment and unemployment, the ways in which participants searched for jobs, the barriers encountered during this process and possible responses to these difficulties. Cycle 1 also contained \textit{Make}. During the workshops, some participants represented their ideas through paper prototypes. These prototypes did not provide complete interface specifications, but their importance was that they allowed ideas to be represented in a tangible form that could be discussed and carried forward into subsequent design activity.

The researcher then synthesised the Cycle 1 findings, participant suggestions and paper prototypes into a more detailed PowerPoint prototype. This was also a form of \textit{Make}, but it was researcher-led. It is important to distinguish this technical and representational development from participant making rather than implying that all artefacts were jointly produced in the same manner. The corresponding \textit{Enact} extended into Cycle 2. During the one-on-one walkthroughs, participants stepped through the PowerPoint prototype and considered how registration, qualifications, profiles, notification choices and other proposed functions would work for them. Although the prototype was not yet a functioning application, the walkthrough enabled participants to place themselves within a possible future interaction with the system.

This enactment immediately generated another \textit{Tell}. Participants identified qualification categories that were missing, requested greater flexibility in self-description, discussed SMS and email, questioned the proposed rating mechanism and commented on naming and interface presentation. One participant also directly redesigned aspects of the interface, providing another example of participant \textit{Make}. The researcher subsequently used this feedback to develop the working web artefact. Cycle 3 then provided the strongest form of \textit{Enact} in the study. Participants registered on and interacted with a functioning artefact and experienced the vacancy-notification service during the two-week deployment. Their comments during the walkthroughs and final evaluation interviews returned the process to \textit{Tell}.

The research can therefore be understood as containing overlapping Tell--Make--Enact loops rather than three independent Tell--Make--Enact cycles. The first loop begins with Tell and Make in Cycle 1 and reaches Enact in Cycle 2. The second develops from the Tell and Make activities in Cycle 2 and reaches Enact in Cycle 3. The Tell arising from Cycle 3 identifies further possibilities for refinement, but another complete empirical cycle was outside the scope of the study. This interpretation provides a more accurate account of the fieldwork than forcing each cycle into a predetermined methodological sequence. Recent South African co-design research similarly illustrates how tangible artefacts can support movement between participants' experiences, emerging ideas and increasingly concrete technical designs \cite{till2025move}.


%------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Co-design Readiness, Group Size and Continuity}

Later South African work on co-design readiness provides a useful retrospective lens through which to interpret some of the methodological difficulties experienced in this study. Till et al. identify emotional, cultural and technological dimensions of readiness and argue that readiness should be fostered rather than assumed \cite{till2026}. This literature was published after the fieldwork and therefore did not inform the original research decisions, but it helps explain why the conditions surrounding a design activity can affect the contribution participants are able to make.

Group size provides one example. At Afrika Tikkun, the researcher had originally intended to conduct the workshop with a small group of approximately five student participants. A trainer changed the arrangement so that 33 student participants took part. The trainer's intention was inclusive, since separating a small group from the rest of the class could itself have been problematic. However, the increase in participant numbers fundamentally changed the research activity. The large workshop allowed many ideas to be generated, and participants could work in groups, discuss their ideas and present conclusions to the researcher. However, the researcher could not hear, question or follow every discussion as it developed. In some cases, what reached the researcher was the conclusion of a subgroup rather than the reasoning through which the conclusion had emerged. The prototyping activity was therefore broader, but the opportunity for detailed engagement with individual participants was reduced.

The corresponding workshop at TZN involved five student participants and enabled closer interaction. The researcher could hear participants' reasoning, ask follow-up questions and engage more directly with the prototypes as they developed. This comparison does not establish that five participants are inherently better than 33. Rather, it demonstrates that participant numbers are themselves a methodological decision. Breadth of participation and depth of engagement may pull in different directions. Increasing the number of participants can increase the number of voices present while simultaneously reducing the opportunity for each voice to influence the interaction in depth.

Participant continuity presented another challenge. The Job Readiness programmes were relatively short, and the circumstances of unemployed participants could change quickly. Participants could obtain work, leave a programme, have family responsibilities, undertake part-time work, or prioritise active job-searching over continued research participation. Co-design can benefit from continuity because participants become familiar with the process and can follow a design from problem identification through refinement and enactment. However, repeated participation should not automatically be treated as an unquestioned methodological good. Dourish et al. draw attention to the affective and practical demands that repeated design participation can place on participants \cite{dourish2020}. This is particularly relevant to unemployed participants because time spent in repeated research sessions can compete with job-searching, paid work, childcare and other responsibilities.

The methodological problem is therefore not simply that participant turnover occurred. A more useful question is how participatory research should be designed when turnover is foreseeable. In such settings, shorter self-contained activities, documentation of previous decisions, deliberate introduction of new participants to the history of the design, and appropriate use of intermediaries may be more realistic than assuming that one stable participant group will remain available throughout an extended research process.


%------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Different Forms of Expertise and the Need to Check Power}

The study also suggests that co-designers should be understood as bringing different forms of expertise rather than as interchangeable participants with identical roles. Job-seekers were experts in their lived experience of searching for work. They understood the costs of travelling to submit CVs, the difficulty of finding relevant opportunities, the qualifications and practical skills they possessed, the communication resources available to them and the experience of responding to recruitment processes. Trainers brought another type of expertise because they worked repeatedly with job-seekers and could identify recurring difficulties across different Job Readiness groups. They also understood the programmes and organisational environments in which participants were receiving employment-support. The researcher brought methodological and technical expertise and was responsible for translating requirements into increasingly concrete artefacts.

These forms of expertise were complementary, but they did not carry identical forms of power. The large AT workshop illustrates this difference. The trainer's organisational authority enabled the trainer to alter the planned structure of the activity. This decision was legitimate within the organisational setting, but it also changed how the research could be facilitated and how directly individual job-seekers could interact with the researcher. Intermediaries can therefore enable research while simultaneously reshaping it. The implication is not that trainers should have less influence. Rather, the researcher needs to examine whether the power exercised by one stakeholder changes the opportunity another stakeholder has to participate.

This concern also applies to the researcher. The researcher controlled the research questions, the time available for the study, the technical implementation and the final synthesis of participant contributions. Hodson et al. argue that participatory design with potentially vulnerable groups requires attention to who participates, when they participate and how power operates across the wider design process \cite{hodson2023}. Applied to this study, the important questions become whose expertise was relevant to a particular decision, who was able to change the conditions of participation, whose contribution was translated into the artefact, and whose absence limited what could be learned.


%------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Researcher Authority and Meaningful Participant Influence}

The researcher was responsible for turning participant contributions into a coherent technical artefact, and this necessarily involved judgement. Participants did not collectively decide every field, technical component or implementation choice. However, the fact that participants did not program the artefact does not mean that their participation was limited to consultation.

There are several instances in which participant contributions produced traceable changes in the design. Qualification categories were expanded after participants identified the absence of N4, N5 and N6 qualifications. Multiple notification channels were retained because participants described differences in email use, data availability and the usefulness of SMS. The proposed rating mechanism was challenged because participants recognised that inexperienced job-seekers could begin with no rating and therefore be disadvantaged. The amount of space available for self-description changed following participant feedback, and participants also influenced interface presentation and naming.

These are stronger indicators of meaningful co-design than whether participants wrote software code themselves. At the same time, researcher synthesis should not be hidden behind the term co-design. The researcher had to combine sometimes conflicting contributions, decide which suggestions could be implemented within the study and translate requirements into technical functions. The methodological contribution is therefore not a claim of equal production. Rather, it is an account of how different forms of knowledge influenced the design while responsibility and power remained unevenly distributed.


%------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Identifying the Right Co-design Parties}

A further retrospective lesson concerns stakeholder identification. The research appropriately began with job-seekers because their experiences of unemployment and job-searching were the immediate concern. Trainers were included because they supported these job-seekers and formed part of the employment-support environment. However, the stakeholders needed to understand a problem are not necessarily identical to those needed once a proposed intervention becomes more concrete.

As the design developed towards an employment platform that could represent job-seekers, identify vacancies and potentially connect job-seekers with employers, employers became consequential stakeholders. Job-seekers could explain what information they wanted represented and how they preferred to receive opportunities. They could not determine what information employers would consider sufficient when evaluating candidates, how employers would search for workers, what would create trust in the platform, or what would motivate employers to use another recruitment channel.

The debate surrounding ratings illustrates this limitation. Some job-seekers recognised that ratings could disadvantage people who had not previously been given an opportunity to establish a reputation. This is an important inclusion concern. An employer might nevertheless require another mechanism through which experience, reliability or previous work could be assessed. Without employer participation, only one side of this tension was explored.

Hodson et al.'s emphasis on considering whom to include and when is therefore particularly relevant \cite{hodson2023}. Stakeholder identification should be revisited as an intervention evolves. Once the decisions or adoption of another group become necessary for the proposed system to achieve its purpose, that group becomes relevant to the co-design process.


%------------------------------------------------
\subsection{When to Fail a Methodology}

These observations lead to a more critical interpretation of methodological success. A methodology should not continue in exactly the same form simply because it was specified at the beginning of a research project. If participant numbers become too large for meaningful interaction, if participant turnover makes the intended type of iterative engagement unrealistic, if an intermediary's organisational power reduces the ability of primary users to participate, or if new stakeholders become necessary but remain absent, then the configuration of the method should be reconsidered.

In this sense, ``failing'' a methodology does not necessarily mean abandoning participatory commitments. It can mean recognising that a particular arrangement is no longer producing the form of participation the research requires and adapting the method accordingly. The present study made some such adaptations. The limitations of large-group engagement became visible through the Cycle 1 workshops, while later cycles increasingly used one-on-one interactions and more concrete prototypes that provided participants with specific design decisions that they could accept, question or change.

The stronger retrospective lesson is that such changes should be understood as methodological decisions rather than merely as logistical adjustments. Co-design readiness, group size, participant burden, continuity, stakeholder composition and the distribution of power should therefore be reviewed throughout a project rather than assumed to remain appropriate once the methodology has been selected. There is also an important balance. High participant turnover should not automatically be interpreted as evidence that co-design has failed. Equally, the desire for continuity should not result in repeatedly requiring the same participants to remain involved merely so that the researcher can complete a preferred methodological sequence. The methodology needs to adapt to participants' circumstances rather than requiring participants to adapt to the convenience of the methodology.

\section{Designing for Job-seekers}

The second argument concerns what the study reveals about the design of employment-support technologies. Participants did not experience unemployment as one simple information problem. Suitable work first had to exist. Job-seekers needed appropriate skills or experience, ways of discovering opportunities, resources with which to search and apply, ways of communicating with employers and ways of presenting their qualifications, skills and experience. Obtaining work was also not necessarily the end of the problem. Some participants had previously left jobs because of low income, transport difficulties, difficult working conditions, the end of contracts or the availability of another opportunity. Employment-support therefore forms a chain of related activities and conditions. A technological intervention can contribute to some parts of this chain but cannot address every part equally.


%------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Why the Employment-Support Platform Was a Reasonable Design Direction}

Cycle 1 did not begin with a predetermined decision to build a job-searching platform. Participants identified different barriers and suggested different responses. These findings resulted in several possible ICT directions, including systems concerned with employment information, skills visibility and other forms of support.

The eventual platform direction was reasonable because it brought together several problems that appeared in the participant data. Participants needed information about available work. Some incurred transport costs while searching for jobs or physically submitting CVs. Preparing and repeatedly submitting employment documents required additional resources. Participants possessed qualifications and practical skills that needed to be represented to potential employers, while they also had different levels of access to email, mobile data and other communication channels.

A platform combining profile information, generated employment documents and relevant vacancy notifications could therefore intervene in several immediate aspects of the job-seeking process. This does not mean that the platform was a technological solution to unemployment. The intervention could not create sufficient employment, remove wider economic constraints, guarantee that an employer would select a particular participant, or ensure that the available work provided sufficient income and acceptable conditions.

Its more immediate potential contribution was narrower: reducing some of the information and transaction costs involved in looking for work. For example, receiving information about a potentially relevant vacancy remotely could reduce the cost associated with repeatedly travelling simply to discover whether an opportunity existed, while generated and reusable employment documents could similarly reduce repeated document preparation. The value of the intervention should therefore be judged against these bounded objectives rather than against whether it could solve unemployment itself.


%------------------------------------------------
\subsection{From Access to Information to Access to Relevant Opportunities}

The findings distinguish between having access to employment information and having access to relevant employment information. An application could present thousands of vacancies and still provide little practical value if the jobs do not correspond with the person's skills, qualifications, location or circumstances. Participant feedback about other job-notification services included frustration with receiving vacancies for which the participant did not qualify. The proposed matching function therefore attempted to reduce the burden of searching by identifying opportunities that corresponded more closely with the job-seeker profile.

This is also why the representation of the job-seeker becomes important. A matching system can only make use of information that the profile contains. Cycle 2 demonstrated how an apparently small design decision could affect this process. N4, N5 and N6 qualifications were not initially represented among the available qualification categories, and participants identified the omission during the PowerPoint walkthroughs. A technically functioning matching mechanism could therefore still exclude relevant opportunities if its representation of the person is incomplete.

During the Cycle 3 deployment, vacancy retrieval and matching were manually performed by the researcher. Participants could experience receiving vacancies that had been selected with reference to their profiles, but the deployment did not evaluate the technical effectiveness of an automated matching algorithm. The study therefore provides evidence about the perceived value of receiving more relevant vacancies. It does not provide evidence that the intended automation could produce equivalent matches reliably at scale.


%------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Qualifications, Practical Skills and Self-representation}

Formal qualifications alone did not capture everything participants could offer. Participants reported different forms of practical experience and skills alongside formal qualifications. For job-seekers whose strongest employment assets may include practical abilities, previous work and informal experience, a design centred only on highest educational qualification risks making part of that experience invisible. The Cycle 2 qualification changes therefore point to a broader design principle: the structure used to represent the job-seeker should reflect locally meaningful forms of qualification and experience rather than assuming that one educational hierarchy is sufficient.

Self-description created a different problem. The Cycle 3 registration process allowed participants to describe their own strengths. Some participants produced clear descriptions, while others experienced difficulty with spelling, grammar or expressing their abilities in written English. Allowing unrestricted text therefore creates space for self-representation but does not guarantee that the resulting description communicates what the job-seeker intends.

The design challenge is not simply to collect more information. Employment-support systems need to help job-seekers represent relevant skills and experience without silently taking ownership of that representation away from them. The role of human support becomes important here. A trainer, researcher or other intermediary may help a job-seeker structure or check a description, but such assistance should remain visible and collaborative. Contemporary automated tools can now assist with the production of CVs and cover letters more easily than was possible when the fieldwork was conducted. However, automation does not remove the underlying problem. The quality of any generated document still depends on accurate information about the person, and the resulting representation still needs to be checked for truthfulness and suitability.


%------------------------------------------------
\subsection{The Cost of Searching for Work}

The findings also show that searching for employment consumes resources. Participants described costs associated with transport, document preparation and connectivity. These barriers were visible in Cycle 1 and appeared again during Cycle 3 when participants compared the application with searching for employment through physical visits or repeated online searches. Earlier South African work on employment-seeking similarly identified the cost and difficulty of accessing employment information among the considerations surrounding mobile job-support \cite{gitau2012,chepken2012tele}.

A digital service can reduce some of these costs, but it can also shift costs from one resource to another. A job-seeker who no longer needs to travel to discover a vacancy may instead require a suitable device, electricity, mobile data or access to a computer. A person who receives a vacancy notification remotely may still require transport to attend an interview or begin employment.

The claim should therefore remain bounded. The artefact could reduce particular search and application costs, but it could not remove all of the financial costs associated with finding and taking up employment.


%------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Communication Should Follow Available Resources}

Participant responses also demonstrated that one communication channel was not appropriate for everybody. Some participants were comfortable using email and internet-based communication, while others particularly valued SMS because an email address or continuous data access could not be assumed. The value of SMS in this study should therefore not be interpreted as a universal claim that SMS is the preferred technology for job-seekers. The more important design finding is the usefulness of providing alternatives.

Earlier ICTD research has demonstrated that communication practices can be distributed across different channels rather than centred on a single technology \cite{densmore2013}. Similarly, research on intermediated and public technology use demonstrates that ownership of a personal device does not necessarily eliminate the usefulness of other access arrangements \cite{sambasivan2010,donner2013}. The implication for employment-support design is therefore not simply ``use SMS''. A service should avoid assuming persistent connectivity through one channel where the intended users may move between mobile data, SMS, email, public computers, NGO computers and other forms of access.


%------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Human Support Remains Part of the System}

The study also challenges the assumption that successful digitalisation should remove human intermediaries. The NGOs and trainers provided computer access, employment preparation, assistance with CVs, interview preparation and other forms of support. Trainers also helped participants understand and engage with some of the research activities. This resembles earlier work showing that technology use in constrained settings may be mediated through other people rather than occurring as entirely independent individual use \cite{sambasivan2010}.

Cycle 3 further demonstrated that access to personal technology did not necessarily remove the value of the organisational environment. At least one participant who had access to personal devices continued to visit the NGO. The study does not establish why that participant returned, and the reason should therefore not be assumed. However, the observation shows that device ownership alone does not describe the full environment in which participants sought employment-support.

The self-description findings provide another example. A person may technically be capable of completing an online form while still benefiting from assistance in expressing experience in a form suitable for recruitment. Human mediation should therefore not automatically be treated as evidence of a poorly designed system. Some users may interact independently, while others may require assistance at particular points. The design challenge is to make such assistance available without creating unnecessary dependency or allowing intermediaries to replace the job-seeker's own voice.


%------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Experience, Reputation and Getting a Foot in the Door}

The findings also expose a tension around employment experience. A job-seeker may possess the ability to perform a task but lack formal evidence of previous employment. However, obtaining some jobs may itself depend on demonstrating previous experience. This creates a circular problem: the person needs employment to establish a record of work but may require an existing work record in order to obtain employment.

The participant responses to the proposed rating system illustrate this problem. A rating could provide an employer with information about previous work performance, and one participant later designed a possible rating scale because she considered this information useful. Other participants raised the opposite concern. An inexperienced job-seeker would begin with no previous rating and could therefore appear less attractive than someone who had already been given opportunities to accumulate ratings.

A design intended to establish trust could therefore reproduce the disadvantage it was intended to overcome. This shows why design features should not be evaluated only according to whether they provide information. Their distributional effects also matter: who benefits from the information, who begins without it and whether the feature reinforces an existing advantage.


%------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Employment Quality Matters}

The findings further suggest that obtaining any job should not automatically be treated as the final indicator of success. Participants' previous employment histories included experiences of low pay, difficult shifts, transport problems and temporary contracts. Some participants continued to seek work despite already having some form of employment.

One participant obtained employment during the two-week Cycle 3 deployment. However, the duration of the deployment was too short to establish that the artefact caused this outcome. The participant also reported that the work eventually performed differed from the role initially described. Since the employer was not a participant in the study, the research cannot establish why this occurred.

The important point is therefore not to speculate about the employer's motive. Instead, the case demonstrates that obtaining employment does not by itself answer questions of job fit, stability, conditions or satisfaction. An employment-support intervention should consequently be evaluated not only according to whether a vacancy was found, but also according to whether opportunities are relevant to the person's skills and circumstances and whether the resulting employment provides a useful progression for the job-seeker.


%------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Design Implications for Employment-support}

Taken together, the findings suggest several principles for employment-support systems intended for users with similar circumstances. Profiles should represent formal qualifications, practical skills and previous employment experience without assuming that one category captures the person's ability. Profiles should also be updateable because skills and experience can change over time. Matching should prioritise relevance rather than simply increasing the number of vacancies shown to the job-seeker, and where matching is automated, the effectiveness and fairness of the matching process need to be evaluated directly.

Communication should allow alternatives where participants have different devices, data resources and communication practices. CV and cover-letter generation can reduce repeated work, but the documents depend on accurate participant information and should preserve the participant's control over how they are represented. Human assistance should remain possible where it adds value in interpretation, digital access or employment preparation.

Finally, employer participation needs to be treated as part of the design problem. A job-seeker profile has little employment value if relevant employers do not see, trust or use the service.

\section{When to Fail a Technology}

The final argument asks a more fundamental question: even where an ICT intervention can be built, is technology the appropriate response to the problem that has been identified? Densmore's account of Claim Mobile provides a useful basis for this question \cite{densmore2012}. Claim Mobile demonstrated that a technology can function technically while changes in context, organisational integration problems or simpler programme responses make continued deployment inappropriate. A functioning prototype therefore does not create an obligation to continue developing or deploying it. The same principle is useful when interpreting Khanya Job Network.


%------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Designable, Supportable and Structural Barriers}

The barriers identified in the study differed in the extent to which technology could address them. Some could be considered \textit{designable in part}. These included discovering vacancy information, reducing repeated searching, preparing reusable employment documents, receiving notifications and organising job-seeker information. Other barriers were \textit{supportable through technology but not fully solvable by it}. These included written self-representation, confidence in navigating recruitment processes, interpretation of opportunities, language difficulties and some aspects of digital skill. Technology could support these activities, but human or organisational assistance could remain important.

A third group was predominantly \textit{structural}. These included the scarcity of appropriate employment, employer hiring decisions, discrimination, inadequate wages, poor working conditions and wider labour-market conditions. Treating all three categories as software requirements would overstate what the artefact could reasonably accomplish.

Receiving a relevant vacancy remotely can reduce the cost of discovering an opportunity, but it cannot create the vacancy. A generated CV can reduce document preparation, but it cannot guarantee that an employer will select the applicant. A matching function may identify work that corresponds with a person's skills, but it cannot ensure that the resulting employment provides adequate pay or acceptable conditions. Recognising these boundaries does not diminish the value of the intervention; rather, it specifies more clearly what kind of contribution the intervention can make.


%------------------------------------------------
\subsection{What the Cycle 3 Deployment Actually Evaluated}

The Cycle 3 deployment also establishes an important boundary around the technical claims that can be made. Registration, profile creation, CV generation, cover-letter generation and the Tips pages were implemented in the working artefact. Vacancy retrieval and matching were not automated.

Instead, during the two-week deployment, the researcher manually performed the process that a future automated component was intended to perform. The researcher searched for vacancies, considered the participant's skills, qualifications and other profile information, checked that the vacancy remained available and then sent relevant information through SMS, email, or both. This was a Wizard of Oz style implementation of the vacancy-retrieval and matching function.

Participants therefore experienced the intended service outcome of receiving selected vacancies, while the underlying retrieval and matching remained human-performed. The deployment consequently provides evidence about participants' experience of receiving relevant notifications. It does not demonstrate the accuracy, reliability or scalability of an automated matching component.

This distinction is particularly important when considering future development. Automating the researcher judgement used during the deployment is itself a substantial design and evaluation problem. A future system would need to retrieve sufficiently current vacancies, interpret job requirements, represent job-seekers accurately and determine relevance without introducing new forms of exclusion.


%------------------------------------------------
\subsection{A Platform Without Employers Is Not Yet an Employment Intervention}

The absence of employers becomes particularly important when considering whether the technology should be taken further. A job-seeker profile produces no employment effect simply by existing online; it has to become visible to somebody with an appropriate opportunity. Likewise, a job-matching service depends either on employers contributing vacancies or on reliable access to external vacancy information.

Employer participation and employer trust are therefore not merely marketing concerns outside the technical design. They are part of the mechanism through which the intervention would be expected to produce value. A technically functional platform with no employer participation could therefore succeed as software while failing as an employment intervention.

Future work would need to examine what employers require from candidate profiles, what information they consider credible, how job-seeker skills should be verified where verification is necessary, how employers prefer to search and communicate, and what would motivate them to use such a system. These questions should not be answered on behalf of employers solely by job-seekers or researchers. They require employer participation while continuing to protect the interests of the job-seekers for whom the intervention was originally designed.


%------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Sustainability Is More Than Keeping the Software Running}

The same issue changes how sustainability should be understood. Keeping the web application online would represent only one part of sustaining the service. Vacancies would need to remain current, matching would need to remain useful, job-seeker profiles would need to change as participants gained qualifications, skills and experience, and users might continue to require support. SMS or other communication mechanisms could create recurring costs, employers or vacancy providers would need to remain engaged, and personal information would have to be managed responsibly.

The two-week deployment did not evaluate these longer-term requirements. Earlier co-design literature associates participation with opportunities for ownership and sustainability \cite{david2013,ssozi2017}. The present findings suggest that this relationship should be treated carefully. Participation in design does not by itself supply the organisational, financial and technical resources required to sustain a service.

A future deployment might therefore involve an NGO, employment organisation, public service or another partner. However, such involvement would need to specify who maintains the system, who supports users, who maintains relationships with employers, who carries communication costs and how the service remains accountable to job-seekers.


%------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Simpler Interventions May Sometimes Be Better}

The findings also create situations in which a non-technological or lower-technology response may be more appropriate than further software development. If the immediate problem is that a job-seeker needs assistance interpreting a vacancy, improved trainer support may sometimes address the problem more directly than another application feature. If the primary difficulty is the cost of receiving information, a concise communication mechanism may be more appropriate than requiring regular access to a feature-rich web application. If the central problem is the absence of employers from the service, establishing and maintaining employer relationships may matter more than adding new job-seeker-facing functionality.

Technology should therefore have to justify its place within the wider employment-support process. It should be retained where it demonstrably reduces a meaningful barrier without creating disproportionate additional burdens. It should be complemented by human or organisational support where software addresses only one part of the problem, and it should be reconsidered where a simpler intervention addresses the underlying problem more directly.

Just as a participatory methodology should not be preserved simply because it was selected at the beginning of a study, a technological intervention should not be preserved simply because it was successfully built.


%------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Managing Expectations}

There is also an ethical dimension to producing an increasingly functional artefact through co-design. During Cycle 3, at least one participant was eager for the application to remain available beyond the research period. The researcher explained that the artefact was being evaluated within the study and would not be permanently deployed as part of the research. The fact that this explanation needed to be repeated shows that expectation management should not be treated as a statement made only once during initial consent.

A participant may interpret an early design idea differently from a functioning application. As an artefact becomes more concrete, expectations concerning its future availability may also change. Ssozi-Mugarura et al. similarly highlight the importance of negotiating expectations in community-based co-design \cite{ssozi2017}.

This is particularly important because participation can create investment in the resulting artefact. Researchers should therefore make clear what will happen at the end of the research, which services will stop, who will retain responsibility for the technology, and what participants should reasonably expect from their involvement.

\section{Synthesis Across the Research Questions}

The discussion above provides a basis for relating the findings directly to the overarching research questions. Rather than treating each question as independent, the answers show how the employment problem, the co-design process and the technological intervention interacted across the three cycles.


%------------------------------------------------
\subsection{RQ1: Barriers Experienced by Participating Job-seekers}

The participating job-seekers experienced a combination of informational, financial, skills-related, technological, experiential and structural barriers. They needed access to information about suitable opportunities, but also faced costs associated with searching, travelling, preparing application documents and maintaining connectivity. Participants differed in formal qualifications, practical skills, digital access and confidence in presenting themselves to employers.

Some barriers could be addressed in part through employment-support technology, while others depended on human support or conditions outside the artefact. The findings therefore show that unemployment and job-seeking should not be treated as one design problem. The different barriers need to be distinguished according to what kind of intervention can reasonably affect them.


%------------------------------------------------
\subsection{RQ2: Contributions of Job-seekers and Intermediaries}

Job-seekers contributed through different Tell, Make and Enact activities. They described their experiences and barriers, proposed responses, created paper representations, critiqued PowerPoint prototypes, influenced specific requirements and later interacted with the functioning artefact. These contributions materially affected decisions concerning qualifications, communication channels, self-representation, interface design and ratings.

Trainers contributed knowledge arising from their repeated experience of supporting job-seekers and also facilitated access to participants. However, trainers did not possess the same expertise as job-seekers, and job-seekers did not possess the same expertise as trainers or the researcher. The contribution of co-design therefore lay in bringing these different forms of expertise into the design process while recognising the differences in authority between them.


%------------------------------------------------
\subsection{RQ3: Evolution of the Artefact Across the Cycles}

The artefact evolved through overlapping Tell--Make--Enact activities rather than through three independent design sequences. Cycle 1 provided problem accounts, candidate responses and participant-generated paper prototypes, which informed the researcher-led development of the PowerPoint prototype. Cycle 2 allowed participants to enact the proposed interaction through one-on-one walkthroughs. Their Tell responses altered qualification representation, communication preferences, self-description, reputation mechanisms and interface presentation, and these contributions informed the development of the working web artefact.

Cycle 3 then allowed participants to interact with implemented functions and experience the two-week vacancy-notification service. The resulting feedback exposed further issues concerning search costs, generated documents, connectivity, self-representation, human support and the limits of the proposed automation. At the same time, contextual conditions shaped this evolution. Participant turnover affected continuity, group size affected depth of engagement, language and technical familiarity influenced some activities, the researcher retained technical responsibility, and employers were not included even though they became increasingly important to the proposed intervention. The resulting artefact therefore reflects both participant contributions and the constraints of the research environment.


%------------------------------------------------
\subsection{RQ4: Possibilities and Limitations of Co-designing ICT for Employment-support}

The study shows that co-design can reveal requirements and tensions that may not be apparent from technical design alone. Participants exposed the importance of locally meaningful qualifications, differences in communication access, potential exclusion caused by reputation mechanisms, the significance of practical skills and the difficulties involved in representing oneself for employment.

However, co-design did not eliminate differences in expertise or power. The researcher retained synthesis and implementation authority, trainers influenced participation conditions, continuity was difficult to maintain, and employers were absent from the design even though their eventual adoption became important. Similarly, ICT could support parts of the employment-search process without resolving unemployment itself. Search, communication and application preparation could be supported technologically, some activities continued to benefit from human mediation, and wider economic and labour-market conditions remained beyond the direct reach of the artefact.

The possibilities of co-designing ICT for employment-support therefore depend on being explicit about both kinds of boundary: what participants can meaningfully influence within the design process, and what the resulting technology can meaningfully influence within the employment problem.


%================================================
\section{Discussion Summary}

Read together, the three cycles suggest that the main contribution of this study is not the technical novelty of Khanya Job Network. The stronger contribution lies in what the process reveals about participation, employment-support and the boundaries of design. The Tell--Make--Enact process shows how different forms of participant knowledge entered the research and how participant contributions could cross the boundaries between formal research cycles. It also demonstrates that meaningful participation depends on more than the presence of participants. Readiness, group size, continuity, participant burden, intermediary power, researcher authority and stakeholder composition all affected what could be achieved.

The resulting artefact demonstrates that selected frictions in job-seeking can be supported through technology. Relevant vacancy information can reduce some searching activity, reusable employment documents can reduce repeated document preparation, and alternative communication channels can accommodate differences in access. However, the deployment also exposed what the study did not establish. Automated vacancy matching was not technically evaluated, employers were not included, long-term sustainability was not examined, the two-week deployment could not demonstrate long-term employment outcomes, and the participant who obtained employment during the deployment cannot be treated as evidence that the artefact caused that outcome.

The resulting position is therefore deliberately bounded. Job-seekers should be treated as experts in their lived experience of seeking work, trainers provide expertise in the support environment, employers become necessary stakeholders where recruitment practices and adoption are involved, and researchers remain responsible for methodology, synthesis and technical implementation. None of these forms of expertise should silently substitute for the others.

Similarly, technology should not be judged only by whether a function can be automated. The more important question is whether the intervention reduces a barrier that matters to the job-seeker, which additional burdens it creates, what human and organisational work remains necessary, and which aspects of the problem remain outside the reach of the artefact. These conclusions provide the basis for Chapter 9, which draws together the contribution and limitations of the study and presents recommendations and directions for future work.
