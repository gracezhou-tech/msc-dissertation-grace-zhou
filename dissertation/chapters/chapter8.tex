\chapter{Discussion}

\begin{chapterintro}
This chapter steps back. Its purpose is not to repeat the findings of Chapters~5 to 7, but to make sense of them across the three cycles, in relation to the research questions and the literature. It asks not only what could be designed, but what should be designed, for whom, under what conditions, and when a methodology or a technology should be reconsidered. The discussion is organised in four parts. The first considers the limits of co-design and what they mean for co-design methodology. The second turns to designing for job seekers, asking which barriers and technologies mattered and what employment-support ICT must take into account. The third asks when to fail a technology, separating the barriers that design can address from those it cannot. The fourth brings the discussion together.
\end{chapterintro}


%================================================
\section{The Limits of Co-design: Implications for Co-design Methodology}

A central methodological lesson from this study is that co-design is conditional. Participation cannot be assessed simply by counting the number of workshops conducted, the number of people present, or whether participants produced prototypes. What is more important is whether participants were able to influence how the problem was understood and how the resulting intervention developed. Across the three research cycles, job seekers described their experiences, identified barriers, suggested possible responses, represented some ideas through paper prototypes, walked through increasingly concrete representations, challenged design assumptions and later interacted with a working artefact. Trainers contributed another form of knowledge based on their continued experience of supporting job seekers, while the researcher remained responsible for facilitating the research, synthesising contributions and developing the technical artefacts.

The study therefore does not understand co-design as a situation in which every participant performs the same activity or exercises equal technical responsibility. Instead, participation is examined in relation to whether the knowledge brought by the different co-designers could influence the understanding of the problem and the decisions made during design. The relevant question is not whether every participant performed every design activity, but whether the method created conditions in which their experiential knowledge could materially influence the design.


%------------------------------------------------
\subsection{\texorpdfstring{\tme}{Tell-Make-Enact} Across the Research Cycles}

The \tme{} activities in this study did not correspond neatly to the boundaries of the three numbered research cycles. Instead, they extended across the cycles as part of an iterative process. This interpretation is consistent with co-design literature in which telling, making and enacting provide different ways through which participants can express experiences, represent ideas and explore possible futures \cite{sanders2008,sanders2014}.

Cycle 1 contained substantial \textit{Tell}. Through interviews and workshops, job seekers and trainers described previous employment, job searching practices, the difficulties participants encountered while looking for work and possible responses to those difficulties. Cycle 1 also contained \textit{Make}. During the workshops, participants represented some of their ideas through paper prototypes. These prototypes did not provide complete interface or functional specifications, but they made some of the participants' proposed responses tangible enough to be discussed, examined and taken forward.

The researcher subsequently synthesised the Cycle 1 findings, participant suggestions and paper prototypes into the more detailed PowerPoint prototype. This was also a form of \textit{Make}, but it was researcher-led. This distinction is important. Researcher development should not be presented as though it were identical to participant making. Participants contributed experiences, problems, possible responses and representations, while the researcher had responsibility for bringing these contributions together into a more concrete artefact.

The corresponding \textit{Enact} extended into Cycle 2. During the one-on-one PowerPoint walkthroughs, participants stepped through how registration, qualifications, profiles, communication choices and other proposed functions would work for them. The PowerPoint prototype was not yet a functioning application, but it allowed participants to place themselves within a possible future interaction and respond to something more concrete than an abstract description.

This enactment immediately generated another \textit{Tell}. Participants identified missing N4, N5 and N6 qualification categories, requested greater flexibility in self-description, discussed SMS and email communication, questioned the proposed rating mechanism, and commented on naming and interface presentation. One participant also directly changed aspects of the proposed interface, providing another participant \textit{Make} contribution. These contributions were subsequently synthesised by the researcher and carried into development of the working web artefact.

Cycle 3 provided the strongest form of \textit{Enact} in the study. Participants registered on and interacted with a functioning artefact and experienced the vacancy-notification service during the two week deployment. Their responses during the initial interactions and final evaluation returned the process to \textit{Tell}. The study can therefore be understood as containing overlapping \tme{} loops. One begins with Tell and Make in Cycle 1 and reaches Enact in Cycle 2. Another develops from the Tell and Make associated with Cycle 2 and reaches Enact in Cycle 3. The Tell arising from Cycle 3 indicates possible further changes, but another empirical cycle was outside the scope of this research.

This interpretation provides a more accurate account than forcing every numbered research cycle into a complete \tme{} sequence. It also demonstrates that a fieldwork cycle and a methodological loop are not necessarily the same unit. An idea can arise and be represented during one participant interaction, be developed between interactions, and only later become sufficiently concrete to enact.

\subsection{Co-design Readiness, Group Size and Continuity}

Later South African research on co-design readiness provides a useful retrospective lens through which to interpret some of the methodological difficulties experienced in this study. Till et al. identify emotional, cultural and technological dimensions of readiness and argue that readiness should be deliberately fostered rather than assumed \cite{till2026}. This work was published after the present fieldwork and therefore did not guide the original research decisions. It does, however, help explain why the conditions surrounding a design activity affect what participants are able or willing to contribute.

Group size provides one example. At Afrika Tikkun, the researcher had intended to conduct the workshop with approximately five student participants. A trainer changed the arrangement so that 33 students participated. The motivation was inclusive, since the trainer did not want a small number of students separated from the rest of the group. However, increasing the number of participants also changed the methodological character of the activity. More people could contribute ideas and participants could work within smaller groups, but the researcher could not hear, probe or follow every discussion as it developed. In some cases, what reached the researcher was a group's conclusion rather than the reasoning through which the group had reached it.

The corresponding TZN workshop involved five student participants and allowed closer interaction. The researcher could hear participants' reasoning, ask follow-up questions and interact more directly with the prototypes as they were being developed. This comparison does not establish that five participants are inherently preferable to 33. Instead, it exposes a trade-off between breadth and depth. Increasing the number of people included can broaden participation while simultaneously reducing the opportunity for detailed engagement with each person's reasoning.

Participant continuity created another methodological tension. The Job Readiness programmes were short, and the circumstances of unemployed job seekers could change quickly. Participants could obtain work, leave the programme, take part-time employment, attend to children or other family responsibilities, or prioritise active job searching over continued research participation. Maintaining one participant group throughout all three cycles was therefore difficult.

Continuity has methodological benefits because participants can become familiar with the design process and observe how their earlier contributions develop. However, repeated participation should not automatically be treated as an unquestioned good. Dourish et al. draw attention to the practical and affective demands that repeated iteration can place on participants \cite{dourish2020}. For unemployed job seekers, continued research participation can compete with the very activities the research is intended to support, including searching for employment, taking temporary work and meeting family responsibilities.

The methodological problem is therefore not simply that participant turnover occurred. A stronger question is how co-design should be structured when turnover is foreseeable. Shorter self-contained engagements, clear documentation of previous decisions, deliberate introduction of new participants to the evolving design and methods that do not depend on one stable group may be more appropriate than assuming continuous participation throughout a long research process.


%------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Different Forms of Expertise and the Need to Check Power}

The study also suggests that co-designers should be understood as contributing different forms of expertise rather than as interchangeable participants. Job seekers were experts in their lived experience of looking for work. They understood the cost of travelling to seek employment, the practical difficulty of repeatedly preparing and distributing CVs, the qualifications and skills they possessed, the communication resources available to them and the experience of interacting with recruitment processes. Trainers contributed knowledge gained from repeatedly supporting different groups of job seekers. The researcher contributed methodological and technical knowledge and was responsible for translating emerging findings and requirements into increasingly concrete artefacts.

These forms of expertise were complementary, but they did not carry identical power. The large AT workshop illustrates this distinction. The trainer's organisational authority made it possible to change the planned workshop arrangement. That decision had understandable organisational motivations, but it also changed the researcher's ability to interact directly with individual job seekers. Intermediaries therefore did not simply enable participation; they could also reshape the conditions under which participation occurred.

The same examination of power needs to be applied to the researcher. The researcher controlled the research questions, available time, methodological decisions, synthesis of evidence and technical implementation. Hodson et al. argue that participatory design with potentially vulnerable groups requires attention to who participates, when participation occurs and how power operates through the process \cite{hodson2023}. Applied to this study, the relevant questions include whose knowledge was relevant to a particular decision, who could change the conditions of participation, whose contribution was translated into the artefact and whose absence restricted what could be learned.


%------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Researcher Authority and Meaningful Participant Influence}

The researcher was responsible for transforming participant contributions into a coherent technical artefact, and this necessarily involved judgement. Participants did not collectively determine every data field, technical component or implementation choice. However, the fact that participants did not program the artefact does not mean that their role was limited to consultation.

Several changes can be traced directly to participant contributions. Qualification categories were expanded after participants identified the absence of N4, N5 and N6 qualifications. Multiple communication options were retained because participants described differences in email use and data access. The proposed rating mechanism was challenged because participants recognised that inexperienced job seekers could begin without ratings and consequently be disadvantaged. The amount of information allowed for self-description changed after participants indicated that the earlier design was too restrictive, while participants also influenced naming and aspects of interface presentation.

These changes are stronger evidence of meaningful co-design than whether participants themselves wrote software. Most of them were additive: they added to the existing design without displacing it, as when qualification categories were extended and the self-description was allowed to be longer. At the same time, researcher synthesis should not be concealed behind the term co-design. The researcher had to combine different and sometimes conflicting contributions, assess technical and practical feasibility and determine how the resulting requirements could be represented within the artefact. Where contributions did conflict, the artefact accommodated both positions where this was possible, as with SMS and email, left a feature unresolved where the disagreement concerned possible harm, as with ratings, and left the decision to the researcher where a practical constraint applied, as with the choice of name, where the concern that ``job market'' could suggest desperation was noted and taken into account (Section~\ref{sec:interface-naming}). Making this authority explicit strengthens the account because it shows both where participant knowledge influenced the design and where researcher responsibility remained.


%------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Identifying the Right Co-design Parties}

The study began appropriately with job seekers because their experience of employment and job searching was the immediate concern. Trainers were also relevant because they supported job seekers and formed part of the employment-support environment surrounding the participants. However, the people required to understand a problem are not necessarily the same people required once a particular intervention direction develops.

As the artefact evolved towards a platform intended to represent job seekers, identify relevant vacancies and potentially connect workers with employers, employers became consequential stakeholders. Job seekers could explain how they wanted their qualifications and skills represented, the barriers they experienced and how they preferred to receive opportunities. They could not establish what information employers would consider sufficient when evaluating workers, what would make employers trust information in the platform, how employers would search for workers or what would motivate employers to adopt the service.

The proposed rating mechanism illustrates this limitation. Job seekers identified the risk that ratings could disadvantage someone who had not previously been given an opportunity to establish a reputation. This is an important inclusion concern. An employer might nevertheless require some other way to assess experience or reliability. Without employer participation, only one side of this tension could be examined.

Stakeholder identification should therefore be revisited as an intervention evolves. The group required to understand the original problem can differ from the group required to make an emerging intervention viable. Once another actor's decisions or adoption become necessary for the intervention to produce its intended effect, that actor becomes relevant to the co-design process.


%------------------------------------------------
\subsection{When to Fail a Methodology}

These observations lead to a more critical interpretation of methodological success. A methodology should not be preserved in exactly the same form simply because it was selected at the beginning of a study. If participant numbers become too large for meaningful interaction, if continuity becomes unrealistic, if an intermediary's organisational authority substantially changes primary-user participation, or if important stakeholders emerge but remain absent, the configuration of the method itself should be reconsidered.

To ``fail'' a methodology in this sense does not necessarily mean abandoning co-design. It can mean recognising that a particular arrangement is no longer producing the form of participation required and changing that arrangement. The movement from broad Cycle 1 workshops towards one-on-one interactions with increasingly concrete prototypes can be interpreted in this way. The later method involved fewer people at one time but created greater opportunity to examine individual preferences, contradictions and reactions to particular design choices.

The stronger retrospective lesson is that co-design readiness, group size, participant burden, continuity, stakeholder composition and the distribution of power should be reviewed throughout a project rather than fixed once at the beginning. High participant turnover should not automatically be interpreted as evidence that co-design has failed. Equally, participants should not be repeatedly retained merely so that a preferred methodological sequence can be completed. The methodology should remain accountable to the circumstances of the people involved.

\section{Designing for Job Seekers}

The second major argument concerns what the study reveals about ICT for job searching. Participants did not experience unemployment as one simple information problem. Suitable work first had to exist. Job seekers then needed information about those opportunities, appropriate qualifications or experience, resources with which to search and apply, ways of communicating with employers and ways of presenting their abilities. Obtaining employment was also not necessarily the end of the problem, since some previous work described by participants involved low pay, unsuitable conditions, temporary contracts or a poor match with their skills.

This makes it important to distinguish the wider employment problem from those parts of job searching that can reasonably become design problems for ICT.


%------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Distinguishing Employment Barriers from Barriers to ICT Use}

An important distinction emerging from the analysis is that the barriers experienced by participants were not all barriers to ICT use. Some were barriers to employment or job searching generally, while others specifically affected participants' ability to use ICT when looking for work. These categories overlap in places, but distinguishing them prevents every difficulty associated with employment from being interpreted as a technology problem.

General employment and job searching barriers included insufficient suitable employment opportunities, lack of required experience or qualifications, difficulty demonstrating practical skills, transport costs, temporary or unsuitable work, employer recruitment decisions, difficulties obtaining additional education and other financial constraints. These conditions formed part of the environment in which participants were seeking employment. Some could be supported indirectly through ICT, but they were not caused by inadequate ICT use and could not necessarily be removed by improving a digital system.

Barriers specifically affecting ICT use included the cost of connectivity, inconsistent access to internet-enabled devices, limited familiarity with searching effectively for vacancies online, differences in email use, language difficulties when interacting with digital systems and, for some participants, the need for assistance with computers or digital forms. One participant struggled to enter information into the working artefact because he was not fluent in English. Another participant without an internet-enabled personal device used the NGO or an internet café to access the internet. The wider research context also included assistance with learning to use computers.

Later South African research provides contemporary support for this distinction. Mangadi and Petersen's study of a data-free South African job search application identifies connectivity, data costs, digital skills, usability and facilitating conditions as continuing influences on whether job seekers can actually use digital employment services \cite{mangadi2024}. Their study was published after the fieldwork reported in this dissertation and therefore did not inform the original design. It nevertheless shows that several of the access conditions encountered in the present research remain relevant to contemporary digital job searching.

There was also overlap between employment and ICT barriers. Limited money could restrict both physical travel and the purchase of mobile data. Language could affect both employability and interaction with a digital system. The distinction is therefore analytical rather than absolute. Its value lies in making clear that RQ2 concerns obstacles affecting ICT-supported job searching specifically, while the broader employment barriers explain the context in which those technologies are being considered.


%------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Why an Employment-support Platform Was a Reasonable Direction}

Cycle 1 did not begin with a predetermined decision to build a job searching platform. Participants identified different difficulties and possible responses, producing ideas including information and funding support, language learning, digital vacancy information, a talent registry and a job searching application.

The eventual employment-support platform was selected because it could bring together several concerns that arose in the data while remaining feasible within the research. Participants needed access to vacancy information, incurred costs while physically searching for work, needed to represent qualifications and practical skills, required employment documents and differed in access to communication channels. A platform that maintained a job seeker profile, generated employment documents and supported delivery of relevant vacancies could intervene in several of these immediate job searching activities within one artefact.

This was nevertheless a bounded design decision. A job searching platform could not create sufficient employment, remove wider economic constraints, provide qualifications that a participant did not possess or guarantee an employer's hiring decision. The selected direction was therefore reasonable because it addressed several immediate and designable barriers identified through the study, not because it represented a complete technological response to unemployment.


%------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Which Technologies Were Appropriate for the Job Seekers?}

The findings do not support the selection of one universally appropriate technology for low-skilled job seekers. Instead, appropriateness depended on the activity being supported and on the resources available to individual participants. The configuration that emerged therefore combined technologies rather than relying on a single communication or interaction channel.

The web-based artefact was useful for activities requiring richer interaction and more information, including creating and maintaining a job seeker profile, representing qualifications and practical skills, generating CVs and cover letters, providing employment guidance and holding the information required for vacancy matching. However, depending entirely on a web interface would have required all participants to have sufficiently regular access to an internet-enabled device, connectivity and the necessary digital familiarity. The fieldwork showed that these conditions could not be assumed.

SMS and email therefore complemented the web artefact rather than duplicating it. During Cycle 2, participants supported having more than one notification channel, including SMS because some job seekers did not regularly use email or have continuous access to data. During Cycle 3, participants could select SMS, email or both. Short vacancy information could therefore reach a participant without requiring repeated searches of a website, while email provided a more appropriate route for longer content such as generated CVs and cover letters.

This combination is usefully interpreted through Densmore et al.'s concept of braided communications \cite{densmore2013}. Their work shows that people and organisations may achieve reliable communication not through one dominant technology, but by using several channels together. The channels coexist, compensate for one another's weaknesses and change as available technologies and practices change. The present findings reflect a similar principle. The question was not whether web, SMS or email should win, but how different channels could support different parts of the job searching process under different access conditions.

Human and organisational support also remained part of the appropriate configuration. Where language, digital familiarity, device access or interpretation created difficulties, trainers and NGO resources remained useful. Later work by Keskinen et al. in a Namibian informal-settlement context similarly shows that digital livelihood opportunities may remain difficult to access where users lack devices, connectivity, language or digital skills, and points towards the value of trained technology mediators \cite{keskinen2022}. Although the type of digital work differs from the present study, the finding strengthens the interpretation that access to a platform does not automatically create the conditions required to use it.

RQ3 therefore does not lead to one device or one platform. For the participants in this study, an appropriate configuration consisted of a lightweight web-based employment-support platform for richer interaction, alternative communication channels according to participant circumstances, and human or organisational support where required. Appropriateness was relational rather than absolute: a technology was appropriate where its capabilities corresponded with both the job searching activity and the resources of the person expected to use it.


%------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Communication Channels in a Changing Technological Environment}

The communication findings also need to be interpreted historically. During the fieldwork, SMS was important because regular mobile data and email use could not be assumed. The study therefore retained SMS alongside email and evaluated both during deployment. The underlying finding was not that SMS would permanently remain the most appropriate communication technology. It was that communication should follow the resources and practices available to job seekers.

The communication environment has changed since the fieldwork. WhatsApp is now widely used in South Africa and would be an important option to investigate in a contemporary implementation. Where a job seeker already has a smartphone and internet access, WhatsApp can carry richer information than SMS, including links, documents and two-way communication. For some users, access to free or public Wi-Fi can also reduce the need to purchase mobile data directly. A contemporary employment-support system could therefore use WhatsApp alongside SMS and email, or allow some users to choose WhatsApp in place of SMS.

This should not be interpreted as evidence that WhatsApp has made SMS universally unnecessary. A job seeker still needs a suitable device and internet connectivity to use WhatsApp, while public Wi-Fi depends on physical access to a hotspot and the conditions under which that service is provided. SMS may remain useful where data connectivity is unavailable. Email may remain appropriate for formal communication or longer documents. The enduring requirement is therefore channel choice rather than allegiance to a particular technology.

This contemporary change also illustrates the value of braided communications \cite{densmore2013}. Communication channels are not static. They coexist and evolve as infrastructure, costs and social practices change. The 2017 implementation used web, SMS and email. A 2026 implementation might braid web access, WhatsApp, email, SMS and public connectivity differently while still responding to the same underlying requirement: job seekers should be able to receive useful employment information without being excluded by an assumption about one communication channel.


%------------------------------------------------
\subsection{From Access to Information to Access to Relevant Opportunities}

The study further distinguishes access to employment information from access to relevant employment information. A system may expose a job seeker to hundreds or thousands of vacancies and still provide little practical value if those vacancies do not correspond with the person's qualifications, practical skills, location or circumstances.

This became particularly visible when a Cycle 3 participant compared the customised notifications with another job-alert service that sometimes supplied vacancies for which the participant did not qualify. The intended matching function therefore attempted to reduce the burden of searching by using information from the job seeker profile to identify more relevant opportunities.

Later South African research also emphasises the importance of usefulness, facilitating conditions and local relevance in digital job search applications \cite{mangadi2024}. This supports the interpretation of the present findings as being concerned with more than the delivery of information. More vacancy information is not necessarily better where the job seeker must still spend time and data filtering opportunities that are clearly unsuitable.

The representation of the job seeker is consequently inseparable from matching. Cycle 2 demonstrated this when participants identified that N4, N5 and N6 qualifications were missing from an early prototype. A matching mechanism can function technically and still produce poor results if its profile categories do not adequately represent the person.

During Cycle 3, vacancy retrieval and matching were manually performed by the researcher. Participants therefore experienced the intended service outcome of receiving selected vacancies, but the study did not evaluate the technical performance of an automated matching algorithm. The evidence supports the usefulness of reducing irrelevant information and providing more targeted opportunities. It does not establish that an automated implementation could reproduce the researcher's judgement accurately, fairly or at scale.


%------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Salient Design Requirements for Employment-support ICT}

The salient design requirements identified across the study reflect issues that remained important as the research moved from understanding the problem to representing a possible intervention and finally to interaction with the working artefact. Their significance is not that every requirement was raised in exactly the same way in every cycle, but that the strongest requirements can be traced through participant experience, design decisions and, in several cases, later evaluation.

One salient requirement was multiple affordable communication channels. Concerns about data and email became explicit during the Cycle 2 walkthroughs, resulting in SMS and email being retained as alternatives. These choices were then implemented during Cycle 3, where participants selected SMS, email or both. The enduring requirement is therefore not specifically SMS, email or WhatsApp, but communication that can adapt to the resources available to a particular job seeker.

A second salient requirement was support for reusable CVs and cover letters. The wider research engagement had already exposed the need for assistance with CV preparation, and document generation became part of the developing artefact. During Cycle 3, several participants particularly appreciated the generated CV format, while another participant valued the cover letter as a shorter introduction to the person than the fuller CV. This requirement therefore developed from an observed need, through implementation, into participant evaluation.

A third salient requirement was support where independent ICT use was difficult. The research context included assistance with computer use and CV preparation. During later interaction, language could still affect a participant's ability to enter information, and some participants relied on NGO or shared facilities for connectivity. The design implication is not that every job seeker requires an intermediary, but that a system intended for this population should not assume that every interaction will occur independently.

A fourth salient requirement was that vacancy information should be relevant rather than merely plentiful. Lack of information was identified early in the research, while later cycles refined the problem towards receiving opportunities that corresponded with the person's profile. Filtering reduces job search effort only when the information used to make that selection is itself sufficiently accurate.

A fifth salient requirement concerned the representation of locally meaningful qualifications, practical skills and employment experience. Cycle 1 showed that participants possessed different combinations of qualifications and abilities. Cycle 2 then exposed limitations in the prototype's qualification categories, including the absence of N4, N5 and N6. Because vacancy relevance depends partly on the information used to describe the job seeker, representation and matching cannot be treated as separate design concerns.

The study also identified a need for appropriate self-representation. A free-text field may technically allow participants to describe themselves, but differences in language and familiarity with recruitment conventions can affect what the resulting profile communicates. An employment-support system can assist with representation, but that assistance should strengthen rather than silently replace the person's own account.

Taken together, these requirements indicate that an appropriate employment-support system is not simply a vacancy database. It needs to consider representation, relevance, communication, application support, accessibility and the possibility of human assistance as connected parts of the same job searching process.


%------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Qualifications, Practical Skills and Self-representation}

Formal qualifications alone did not capture everything participants could offer. Participants described practical skills and previous employment alongside educational qualifications. For job seekers whose strongest assets may include driving, cooking, cleaning, hairdressing or other practical abilities, a system centred only on the highest formal qualification risks making valuable experience invisible.

The Cycle 2 changes to qualification categories therefore point towards a broader design issue. The data structure used to describe a job seeker can influence the opportunities for which that person becomes visible. Locally meaningful qualifications and practical skills should be represented in ways that do not force participants into categories that inadequately describe them.

Self-description produced a different difficulty. Participants were asked to describe personal strengths, but some descriptions demonstrated the difficulty of translating lived experience and personal qualities into formal recruitment language. Providing a text box therefore creates an opportunity to speak but does not guarantee that the resulting text communicates what the person intends.

The appropriate response is not simply to automate the participant's voice away. A trainer, intermediary or contemporary language-support tool may help a job seeker organise or check an application, but the person should remain able to verify and control what is represented. This becomes particularly important as AI-assisted CV and cover-letter tools become more readily available. Easier generation does not remove the need for accurate information, transparency and control by the job seeker.


%------------------------------------------------
\subsection{The Cost of Searching for Work}

Job searching consumed resources that were already limited. Participants described transport costs, connectivity costs and the repeated work involved in preparing or distributing employment documents. These concerns appeared during the earlier research and were reinforced during Cycle 3 when participants compared the artefact with door-to-door searching and repeated CV preparation.

The findings suggest that the cost of a digital intervention should not be examined separately from the wider cost of the activity it replaces. A digital system may require data while reducing transport. A generated CV may require connectivity while reducing repeated preparation or printing. A vacancy notification may create a communication cost while reducing the need to search numerous sites. The relevant design question is therefore whether the intervention reduces the overall burden of job searching rather than whether the technology itself is completely cost free.

Mangadi and Petersen's later South African findings on data costs, connectivity and facilitating conditions strengthen this point \cite{mangadi2024}. A data-free or low-data application can remove one access barrier, but usefulness still depends on usability, relevant content and the wider conditions under which a job seeker interacts with the technology.

The contribution of the artefact should therefore remain bounded. It may reduce particular transaction and search costs, but it cannot remove every cost involved in obtaining employment. A participant who discovers a vacancy remotely may still require transport to attend an interview or reach the eventual workplace.


%------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Human Support Remains Part of the System}
\label{sec:human-support}

The study challenges the assumption that successful digitalisation should remove human intermediaries. The NGOs and trainers provided more than access to computers. They supported CV preparation, computer learning, interview preparation, job-readiness activities and other aspects of seeking employment. These forms of assistance formed part of the environment in which the technological intervention was being designed.

The Cycle 3 findings reinforced this point. Participants sometimes used NGO computers or other shared access points, and one participant had difficulty entering information because of language. Access to technology did not automatically remove the need for interpretation or assistance.

The later work of Keskinen et al. provides useful regional support for this finding \cite{keskinen2022}. In their study of digital microwork in a Namibian informal settlement, access depended not simply on whether a digital work platform existed, but also on resources and capabilities such as devices, internet connectivity, digital literacy, English and other supporting infrastructure. Their discussion of technology mediators is particularly relevant to the present finding that a technologically available service may still require human support before it becomes practically usable.

This does not mean that every job seeker should be required to work through an intermediary. The design challenge is to allow different degrees of independence. A confident and connected participant should be able to proceed without unnecessary mediation, while a participant who needs assistance should not be excluded simply because completely independent ICT use has been assumed.

Human support should therefore be understood as one possible component of an employment-support system rather than automatically as evidence that the technology has failed.


%------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Experience, Reputation and Getting a Foot in the Door}

The proposed rating mechanism exposed another tension within employment-support design. Job seekers may require employment experience in order to demonstrate reliability, while obtaining the first opportunity may itself depend on evidence of previous experience.

A reputation mechanism could provide employers with information about previous work performance, and one participant later proposed a possible rating scale. Other participants, however, recognised that a person entering the labour market would begin without ratings and could consequently appear less attractive than somebody who had already accumulated a work history.

A feature intended to increase trust could therefore reproduce an existing disadvantage. This illustrates why features should not be evaluated only according to the information they make available. Their effects on different categories of job seeker also matter. Future mechanisms involving references, ratings or verified skills would need to provide employers with useful information without making previous opportunity a prerequisite for future opportunity.


%------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Employment Quality Matters}
\label{sec:employment-quality}

The findings also show that obtaining any job should not automatically be treated as the final indicator of success. Participants' previous employment histories included concerns about low pay, transport, temporary contracts and dissatisfaction with particular roles. Some participants who were already employed were still looking for different work.

One participant obtained employment during the Cycle 3 deployment. However, the two week period was too short to establish that the artefact caused that outcome. The participant also reported that the work eventually performed differed from the role initially described. Since the employer was not a participant in the research, the study cannot establish why this occurred.

The appropriate conclusion is therefore not to speculate about the employer's motive. Instead, this case demonstrates that employment outcomes need to be interpreted in relation to job fit, stability, working conditions and progression rather than merely whether a person moved from unemployment into any available role.

\section{When to Fail a Technology}

The final argument asks whether a technological intervention should continue simply because it can be built. Densmore's account of Claim Mobile is useful here because it demonstrates that a functioning technology can still become inappropriate where context changes, organisational integration is problematic or simpler programme responses address the underlying issue more effectively \cite{densmore2012}.

Khanya Job Network should therefore be evaluated not only according to whether its software functions operated, but according to which barriers those functions could reasonably address, what burdens they reduced or introduced, and what additional human and organisational work would be required if the intervention moved beyond the research setting.


%------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Designable, Supportable and Structural Barriers}
\label{sec:designable-barriers}

The barriers identified in the research differed in the extent to which technology could address them. Some were designable in part. These included discovering vacancy information, reducing repeated searching, preparing reusable employment documents, receiving notifications and organising job seeker information.

Other barriers could be supported through technology but were not fully solvable by it. These included self-representation, language, interpretation of recruitment information, confidence and some aspects of digital skill. Technology could assist, but human or organisational support could remain valuable.

A third set was predominantly structural. These included the scarcity of suitable employment, employer hiring decisions, discrimination, inadequate wages, insecure working conditions and wider labour-market conditions. Treating all three categories as software requirements would exaggerate what an employment-support artefact could reasonably accomplish.

A vacancy notification can reduce the cost of discovering an opportunity but cannot create the vacancy. A generated CV can reduce repeated document preparation but cannot guarantee that an employer will select the applicant. A matching mechanism can identify work that appears compatible with the information supplied by a job seeker, but it cannot guarantee adequate pay, acceptable working conditions or long-term employment.

Recognising these boundaries does not diminish the value of ICT. It specifies more accurately where the intervention may contribute and where another form of response is required.


%------------------------------------------------
\subsection{What the Cycle 3 Deployment Actually Evaluated}

The two week deployment creates a further boundary around the technical conclusions. Registration, profile creation, CV generation, cover-letter generation and employment Tips were implemented in the working artefact. Vacancy retrieval and matching were not automated.

Instead, the researcher manually performed the process that a future automated component was intended to undertake. Vacancies were searched for, compared with information in participant profiles, checked for continuing availability and then communicated by SMS, email or both. Participants did not see this underlying manual work and therefore experienced the service outcome without being aware that the matching itself was human-performed.

The deployment can consequently tell us about participants' experience of receiving selected vacancy information. It cannot establish the accuracy, fairness, reliability or scalability of an automated matching mechanism. Automating the researcher's judgement would itself require another design and evaluation process.

This distinction makes visible the human labour that can sit behind an apparently automated service. During the study, relevance depended on human interpretation of both vacancy descriptions and job seeker profiles. Replacing that interpretation with software should therefore not be treated as a small implementation step. It is a separate research problem involving representation, matching accuracy, current vacancy information and the possibility of new forms of exclusion.


%------------------------------------------------
\subsection{A Platform Without Employers Is Not Yet a Complete Employment Intervention}

The absence of employers becomes increasingly important once the artefact is interpreted as an employment platform rather than solely as a job seeker support tool. A job seeker profile produces no employment effect simply by existing online. Somebody with an appropriate opportunity needs to see, trust or act on that information.

Similarly, a matching service depends on access to credible and current vacancies. Employer participation and employer trust are therefore not external marketing concerns. They form part of the mechanism through which the proposed intervention would eventually be expected to produce employment-related value.

A platform can therefore succeed technically while still failing as an employment intervention if the employer side of the relationship is absent. Future work needs to examine what employers require from candidate profiles, how skills and experience should be represented, what information they consider credible, how employers prefer to communicate and what would motivate them to participate.

The omission of employers is therefore both a limitation and a methodological finding. As the artefact evolved, the set of stakeholders required to understand and sustain it also changed.


%------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Sustainability Is More Than Keeping the Software Running}

Sustainability similarly extends beyond hosting the application. Vacancies would need to remain current, profiles would need to be updated, communication channels could create recurring costs, job seekers might continue to require support, employer relationships would need to be maintained, and personal information would need to be managed securely.

The two week deployment did not evaluate these longer-term requirements. Earlier co-design literature associates participation with possibilities for ownership and sustainability \cite{david2013,ssozi2017}, but the present findings suggest that participation alone cannot supply the organisational, financial and technical resources required to maintain an employment service.

A future implementation might involve an NGO, public employment service, employer network or another organisation, but those responsibilities would need to be explicit. Sustainability therefore becomes another design problem: who maintains the system, who supports users, who carries communication costs, who maintains access to vacancies, who protects participant information and who remains accountable to job seekers?


%------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Simpler Interventions May Sometimes Be Better}

The study also identifies circumstances in which further software development may not be the most appropriate response. If the immediate difficulty is interpreting a vacancy, trainer assistance may address the problem more directly than another application feature. If the problem is simply receiving information affordably, a concise notification mechanism may be more appropriate than requiring repeated interaction with a feature-rich web application. If the central limitation is the absence of employers, developing employer relationships may matter more than adding further job seeker facing functionality.

Technology should therefore have to justify its place within the wider employment-support process. It should be retained where it reduces a meaningful barrier without creating disproportionate new burdens, complemented by human or organisational support where software addresses only part of the problem, and reconsidered where another intervention addresses the underlying difficulty more directly.

Just as a participatory methodology should not be preserved simply because it was selected at the beginning of a study, a technological intervention should not be preserved simply because it was successfully built.


%------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Managing Expectations}

There is also an ethical implication to developing a functioning artefact through co-design. During the research, at least one participant expected or hoped that the system would remain available after the study even after the limited scope of the deployment had been explained. This demonstrates that participant expectations can change as an idea becomes increasingly concrete.

Expectation management should therefore not be treated as a statement made only during initial consent. Participants may interpret an early prototype differently from a functioning application that appears capable of continuing beyond the research period. Ssozi-Mugarura et al. similarly discuss the importance of negotiating expectations within community co-design \cite{ssozi2017}.

Researchers should therefore communicate throughout a project what will happen to the intervention after the research, which services will continue or stop, who will retain responsibility for the technology and what participants should reasonably expect from their contribution.

\section{Discussion Synthesis}

Read together, the research questions form a progression. RQ1 concerns how participants were already using ICT in job searching. RQ2 examines the barriers that limited that use. RQ3 asks which technologies were appropriate under those conditions. RQ4 moves from the technology configuration to the design requirements that emerged through the cycles, while RQ5 turns the same critical attention towards the co-design process and asks what methodological trade-offs were encountered.

The findings show that job seekers used ICT alongside personal networks, physical searching, NGO resources and other forms of support. Barriers to ICT use overlapped with, but were not identical to, the wider barriers associated with employment. The technologies that emerged as appropriate therefore formed a combination rather than a single solution. The web artefact, SMS, email and human support each performed different roles, while contemporary technologies such as WhatsApp demonstrate that the particular components of this combination can change even when the underlying principle remains the same.

The co-design process was similarly layered. Different forms of participation became possible at different stages, \tme{} activities crossed research-cycle boundaries, intermediaries could simultaneously enable and reshape participation, and researcher synthesis remained necessary. Meaningful participation was therefore not demonstrated through equal technical production but through the traceable influence of participant knowledge on the developing design.

The resulting position is deliberately bounded. Job seekers should be treated as experts in their experience of seeking work, trainers as experts in the support environment, employers as necessary stakeholders where recruitment and adoption are involved, and researchers as responsible for methodology, synthesis and technical implementation. None of these forms of expertise should silently substitute for the others.

Technology should be treated with the same restraint. Some search, communication and application burdens are open to design. Other needs continue to require human and organisational support. Still others arise from the scarcity and conditions of employment and remain outside the direct reach of an artefact. These conclusions provide the basis for Chapter 9, which returns directly to the five research questions, states the contributions and limitations of the dissertation, and identifies what should be carried forward from the study.
