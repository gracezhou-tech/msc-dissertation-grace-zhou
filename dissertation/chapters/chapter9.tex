\chapter{Conclusion}

\begin{chapterintro}
This dissertation began with unemployed job-seekers describing the ordinary but consequential difficulties involved in looking for work: finding relevant opportunities, travelling to submit CVs, preparing employment documents, representing qualifications and practical skills, paying for communication and connectivity, and navigating systems that did not always reflect their circumstances. From these accounts, the research moved through paper prototypes, a PowerPoint prototype, a working web artefact and finally a two-week deployment. What began as an investigation of possible ICT support therefore also became an investigation of participation itself: who is able to contribute, in what way, under what conditions, and how those contributions become part of a technological design.

This final chapter returns to the aim and research questions, draws together the principal findings of the three cycles, identifies the contributions of the dissertation, reflects on what was learned from the research process, and defines the boundaries within which the findings should be understood. It concludes with recommendations for future research and development. The central position that emerges is deliberately bounded: co-design can help reveal what matters to job-seekers and can materially shape an ICT intervention, while ICT can reduce some of the frictions involved in job-seeking. Neither co-design nor technology, however, should be asked to carry more than it can reasonably bear.
\end{chapterintro}

\section{Returning to the Research Aim}

The aim of this dissertation was to investigate how co-design with job-seekers and intermediaries could reveal barriers to employment-related activities and inform the design and refinement of an ICT intervention in the study context. The research achieved this aim through three iterative cycles conducted at Afrika Tikkun and The Zanokhanyo Network. Cycle 1 concentrated on understanding participants' experiences, barriers and possible responses. Cycle 2 made the emerging design more concrete through one-on-one walkthroughs of a PowerPoint prototype. Cycle 3 moved into interaction with a working web artefact and a two-week deployment.

The study did not demonstrate that technology can solve unemployment, nor was that ultimately the most useful question to ask. The stronger finding is that unemployment and job-seeking need to be separated analytically. The number and quality of jobs available are shaped by economic, institutional and labour-market conditions beyond the reach of an application. The process of seeking those jobs, however, contains smaller points of friction that can sometimes be redesigned. Information can be made easier to reach, repeated document preparation can be reduced, communication can be adapted to available resources, locally meaningful qualifications can be represented more accurately, and job opportunities can potentially be filtered so that job-seekers spend less effort on clearly unsuitable vacancies.

The contribution of the artefact therefore lies not in claiming to solve unemployment, but in testing how some of these more immediate frictions might be reduced while making visible the barriers that technology cannot remove.

\section{What the Study Found}

Across the three cycles, the study found that the employment difficulties described by participants were layered rather than singular. Some concerned access to information: participants needed to know that relevant opportunities existed and how to find them. Others concerned the cost of searching, including transport, connectivity and the preparation or submission of employment documents. Other difficulties concerned qualifications, practical skills, previous experience and the ability to represent these convincingly to an employer. Participants also differed in their access to devices, email, mobile data and other communication channels. Beyond these lay conditions that neither the participants nor the artefact could directly change, including the availability of suitable work, employer decisions, remuneration and working conditions.

This distinction became increasingly important as the research progressed. In the early stages, it was tempting to treat the identified barriers as possible requirements for a technological solution. The later analysis showed that this would have been too broad. Some barriers were directly designable in part, some could be supported through technology while continuing to require human assistance, and some remained predominantly structural. This layered interpretation is one of the most important lessons of the dissertation because it places boundaries around what counts as an appropriate ICT response.

The study also found that access to employment information is not equivalent to access to a relevant opportunity. A job-seeker may have access to hundreds or thousands of vacancies and still remain poorly served if those vacancies do not correspond with the person's qualifications, skills, experience, location or circumstances. The Cycle 2 omission of N4, N5 and N6 qualifications from an early representation was therefore more than a small interface problem. It demonstrated how the categories through which a system represents a job-seeker can determine whether that person becomes visible to a matching process at all.

A similar issue emerged around practical skills and self-representation. Formal educational qualifications did not capture everything participants could do. Participants described different forms of practical experience and previous work, while the self-description activity showed that having the opportunity to enter free text did not necessarily mean that every participant could present their strengths in a form suited to formal recruitment. An employment-support system therefore needs to support representation, not merely data collection.

The findings concerning communication led to another important conclusion. There was no single communication mechanism that was appropriate for all participants. SMS, email, web access and organisational computer access had different advantages under different circumstances. Earlier work on mobile and intermediated ICT use similarly emphasised that access often occurs through combinations of devices, channels and people rather than one permanently available technology \cite{densmore2013,sambasivan2010,donner2013}. The contribution of the present study is therefore not the simple recommendation that employment-support systems should use SMS. It is the broader principle that communication design should follow the resources available to the job-seeker rather than require the job-seeker to conform to one assumed mode of access.

Human assistance also remained significant. The trainers and NGO environments did more than provide computers. They were part of the employment-support ecology in which participants prepared CVs, learned employment practices, interpreted information and sought assistance. The findings therefore reinforce earlier work showing that intermediation can remain important even where digital technologies are available \cite{sambasivan2010}. Technology and human support did not appear as opposites in this study. In several instances, they were part of the same support process.

Finally, the study found that obtaining a job is itself too narrow a measure of success. Participants' histories included concerns about wages, transport, temporary employment and working conditions. One participant obtained employment during the two-week deployment, but the duration and design of the study do not allow that outcome to be attributed causally to the artefact. Furthermore, the work eventually undertaken differed from the role initially described. The lesson is that an employment-support intervention should ultimately be concerned not simply with whether any employment occurs, but with whether job-seekers gain access to opportunities that are relevant, viable and capable of supporting meaningful progression.

\section{Answering the Research Questions}

Because this is the final point at which the four research questions are brought together, they are answered directly below. The answers are necessarily bounded by the qualitative and situated nature of the study, but they are supported by evidence accumulated across the three research cycles.


%------------------------------------------------
\subsection{RQ1: What barriers to employment and job-seeking are experienced by the participating job-seekers in the two study settings?}

The study found that the participating job-seekers experienced interconnected informational, financial, skills-related, technological, experiential and structural barriers. Participants needed to find relevant opportunities and understand how to access them, but searching itself consumed scarce resources through transport, data, communication and document preparation. Some participants possessed practical skills or previous experience that were not adequately represented through formal qualification categories, while others encountered difficulties related to written self-presentation, language or familiarity with digital recruitment practices.

These barriers did not all belong to the same level of the employment problem. Some concerned activities that an ICT intervention could directly support, such as obtaining vacancy information, preparing reusable employment documents or receiving notifications. Others, including interpretation, self-presentation and aspects of digital confidence, could be assisted by ICT but continued to benefit from human support. Still others, such as insufficient suitable vacancies, employer hiring decisions, wages and working conditions, remained outside the direct reach of the artefact.

RQ1 is therefore answered by rejecting a single explanation of the participants' employment difficulties. The participants experienced a chain of barriers, and an appropriate intervention first requires distinguishing which link in that chain is being addressed.


%------------------------------------------------
\subsection{RQ2: How do job-seekers and intermediaries contribute to identifying, designing and refining possible ICT responses to those barriers?}

Job-seekers and trainers contributed different but complementary forms of expertise. Job-seekers contributed first-hand knowledge of looking for work, paying the costs of job-seeking, representing their skills, using communication technologies and encountering recruitment requirements. Trainers contributed accumulated knowledge gained through repeatedly supporting job-seekers within the Job Readiness programmes. The researcher contributed methodological and technical expertise and retained responsibility for synthesis and implementation.

These contributions occurred through different forms of Tell, Make and Enact. Participants told the researcher about their experiences and ideas; some made paper representations or altered proposed designs; participants enacted possible future interactions through PowerPoint walkthroughs and later through use of the working artefact. Their contributions led to traceable changes, including the representation of N4, N5 and N6 qualifications, greater flexibility in self-description, communication choices, interface changes and reconsideration of a reputation mechanism that could disadvantage inexperienced job-seekers.

The answer to RQ2 is therefore not that every participant designed every part of the artefact. Meaningful participation occurred when experiential knowledge had consequences for problem framing, requirements or design decisions. This distinction is important because participation should not be equated with programming ability or equal technical production. It should instead be examined through influence.


%------------------------------------------------
\subsection{RQ3: How do participant contributions and contextual constraints shape the requirements and evolution of the artefact across the three co-design cycles?}

The artefact evolved through an iterative process in which Tell, Make and Enact crossed the boundaries of the three numbered research cycles. Cycle 1 contained substantial Tell as participants described barriers, experiences and possible responses, together with participant Make through the production of paper prototypes. The researcher then synthesised these contributions into the PowerPoint prototype. Its Enact occurred in Cycle 2 when participants stepped through the proposed interaction and responded to increasingly concrete representations of registration, profiles and functions.

That enactment generated another Tell. Participants identified what was missing, unsuitable or potentially exclusionary, and some contributions also became further Make. The researcher subsequently incorporated these inputs into the working web artefact. Cycle 3 provided the strongest Enact because participants interacted with implemented functions and experienced the two-week vacancy-notification service, after which their feedback generated another Tell that could have informed a subsequent design cycle.

At the same time, the artefact was shaped by constraints. The same participants could not be maintained throughout the full study. Group size altered the quality of interaction. Trainers could influence the practical conditions under which research occurred. Participants differed in language confidence and familiarity with technology. Technical feasibility and available time affected what the researcher could implement. The researcher also retained final responsibility for translating participant contributions into the artefact.

RQ3 is therefore answered by showing that the artefact did not evolve through requirements alone. It developed through an interaction between participant knowledge, researcher judgement and contextual constraint. Co-design shaped the artefact, but the context also shaped what co-design itself could become.


%------------------------------------------------
\subsection{RQ4: What does the study reveal about the possibilities and limitations of co-designing ICT for employment-related challenges in this context?}

The study shows that co-design can make visible needs, tensions and design assumptions that may otherwise remain hidden. Participants identified locally meaningful qualifications, differences in communication access, the importance of representing practical skills and the potential exclusionary effect of reputation mechanisms. Co-design also allowed the intervention to change as participants encountered progressively more concrete representations rather than requiring all requirements to be known at the beginning.

Its limitations were equally instructive. Participation was uneven, the same people could not remain throughout all cycles, group size changed the depth of interaction, intermediaries exercised organisational power, the researcher retained methodological and technical authority, and employers were absent even after their participation became important to the functioning of the proposed platform. Later South African work on co-design readiness provides a useful retrospective explanation for why the conditions around participation matter rather than readiness simply being assumed \cite{till2026}. Dourish et al. further caution against treating repeated participation itself as an uncomplicated good \cite{dourish2020}.

The ICT intervention had similar boundaries. It could potentially reduce some job-search, communication and document-preparation burdens, but it could not create jobs or alter many of the structural conditions producing unemployment. Vacancy matching was manually simulated during Cycle 3, meaning that participants experienced the intended service but the automated matching mechanism itself was not evaluated.

RQ4 is therefore answered through a double boundary: co-design can meaningfully shape an employment-support intervention without eliminating unequal power, and technology can usefully address selected parts of job-seeking without becoming a solution to unemployment itself.

\section{Contributions of the Dissertation}

The contribution of this dissertation does not rest on claiming that every individual finding is unprecedented. Several findings reinforce concerns already present in participatory design and ICTD literature, including the importance of context, intermediaries, affordable communication, participant influence and the limits of technological intervention. The contribution lies partly in confirming how these concerns appeared in this particular South African employment-support setting and partly in the new analytical relationships that became visible when the three cycles were considered together.


%------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Contribution 1: Participation Through Influence}

The first contribution is a way of assessing participation through demonstrable influence rather than equal technical production. Existing co-design literature recognises making and participation as broader than software implementation \cite{sanders2008,sanders2014}. The present study gives this principle a concrete empirical form. Job-seekers did not need to program the web artefact for their contribution to matter. Their participation became visible where their experiences changed the categories, communication mechanisms, representations or assumptions embodied in the design.

This leads to a practical criterion for analysing similar projects:

\begin{quote}
The relevant question is not whether every participant performed every design activity, but whether the method created conditions in which their experiential knowledge could materially influence the design.
\end{quote}

This framing also makes researcher authority easier to discuss honestly. The researcher synthesised competing contributions, selected feasible directions and implemented the artefact. Rather than weakening the account of co-design, making this asymmetry visible allows the dissertation to show more precisely where participants influenced the design and where researcher judgement remained decisive.


%------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Contribution 2: Tell--Make--Enact as a Process Across Cycles}

A second contribution is the observation that Tell, Make and Enact need not be contained within identical, self-sufficient research cycles. In this study, the first Tell and Make activities occurred in Cycle 1, while the related Enact occurred when participants walked through the resulting PowerPoint representation in Cycle 2. The Tell generated during that enactment informed further Make and led to the working artefact, whose Enact occurred in Cycle 3.

This cross-cycle interpretation provides a more faithful account of the fieldwork than retrospectively forcing each cycle into Tell followed by Make followed by Enact. It suggests that the named research cycle and the methodological loop are not necessarily the same unit. A methodological activity can begin in one participant encounter, continue through researcher synthesis between encounters, and only become enactable when a sufficiently concrete representation is available.

The contribution is therefore not a new Tell--Make--Enact framework, but a situated demonstration of how such activities can overlap, recur and cross formal cycle boundaries in a constrained longitudinal co-design process.


%------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Contribution 3: When to Fail a Methodology}

A third contribution arises from the difficulties encountered in implementing co-design. These difficulties initially appeared as limitations: participant turnover, a workshop that became substantially larger than planned, differences between job-seekers and trainers, uneven familiarity with design activities, and the researcher's continued authority. Considered together, however, they produce a stronger methodological lesson.

A participatory methodology should not be preserved in exactly the same form merely because it was selected at the beginning of the research. When a group becomes too large for meaningful engagement, when participant continuity becomes unrealistic, when an intermediary changes the conditions of participation, or when new stakeholders become necessary, the configuration of the methodology should be open to change.

To ``fail'' a methodology in this sense is not to abandon participation. It is to refuse to preserve a particular methodological arrangement after it ceases to produce the form of participation intended. The movement towards one-on-one prototype walkthroughs in later cycles can therefore be understood not as less co-design, but as an adaptation that enabled more focused participant engagement.

This argument extends the reflexive position developed in Chapter 8 and parallels Densmore's argument that a functioning technology should still be abandoned when it no longer appropriately serves the problem \cite{densmore2012}. Methodologies, like technologies, should remain accountable to the contexts in which they are being used.


%------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Contribution 4: Distinguishing Designable, Supportable and Structural Employment Barriers}

A fourth contribution is the distinction between barriers that are directly designable in part, barriers that are supportable through ICT but continue to require other forms of assistance, and predominantly structural barriers.

This distinction emerged from the need to make sense of the wide range of issues participants raised. Vacancy discovery, notification and document preparation could be directly affected by system design. Self-representation, language, confidence and interpretation could be technologically supported but were not necessarily best addressed through automation alone. Job scarcity, wages, employer decisions and wider labour-market inequality were not problems that the artefact could directly resolve.

This classification contributes an analytical boundary that is useful beyond the specific artefact. It discourages the conversion of every social difficulty into a software feature and instead asks what relationship a particular barrier actually has to technology. It therefore places the intervention within, rather than above, the employment environment in which it operates.


%------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Contribution 5: Stakeholders Can Emerge as the Artefact Evolves}

A fifth contribution concerns stakeholder identification. The job-seekers and trainers were appropriate participants for understanding the immediate problems around job-seeking. However, once the intervention developed towards a platform whose usefulness depended upon employers recognising, trusting or acting on job-seeker information, employers became consequential stakeholders.

This suggests that stakeholder identification should not necessarily be completed once at the beginning of a co-design study. The relevant co-design parties can change as the proposed intervention changes. The people needed to understand a problem may differ from the people needed to make a particular response work.

The absence of employers is therefore both a limitation of this study and a methodological finding. It shows why stakeholder composition needs to be reviewed as the intervention becomes more concrete.


%------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Contribution 6: Making the Hidden Human Work of Automation Visible}

The Cycle 3 vacancy-notification process provides a final design contribution. Participants experienced vacancies selected according to information in their profiles, but the underlying vacancy retrieval and matching were performed manually by the researcher. The deployment therefore simulated the service that a future automated component was intended to provide.

This makes visible an important distinction between an automated interface and the human work required to produce its output. During the study, relevance depended on the researcher's judgement: searching vacancies, interpreting requirements, comparing them with participant information and checking that opportunities remained current. Replacing that work with an algorithm would not simply be a technical completion of an already validated feature. It would introduce a new research problem concerning representation, matching accuracy, fairness, currency and exclusion.

The study therefore contributes a clearer boundary around what its working artefact demonstrated. It evaluated the participant experience of a proposed matching service, not an automated matching system.

\section{What Was Confirmed from Earlier Work}

Not all worthwhile research contributions need to contradict existing literature. Several findings in this study reinforce earlier work and show its continuing relevance in this context.

First, the importance of multiple communication channels is consistent with research showing that communication in constrained settings can be braided across different technologies rather than centred on one permanent channel \cite{densmore2013}. The present study similarly found value in allowing job-seekers to move between SMS, email, web access and organisational resources.

Second, the continued role of trainers and NGO spaces reinforces earlier observations of intermediated technology use \cite{sambasivan2010}. Access to a device did not necessarily remove the need for assistance, interpretation or institutional support.

Third, the findings concerning the costs of seeking employment and the potential usefulness of mobile employment support resonate with earlier South African work such as Ummeli \cite{gitau2012}. The contribution of the present study is not merely that such costs existed, but that these costs were followed through a co-design process into specific design decisions and subsequently reconsidered during deployment.

Fourth, the difficulties and adaptations encountered during co-design align with more recent work on co-design readiness and the importance of deliberately establishing conditions for meaningful participation \cite{till2026}. Because this literature post-dates the fieldwork, it is used here as a retrospective interpretive lens rather than as a claim that it informed the original research decisions.

The findings therefore sit in dialogue with both older and newer work. Some confirm patterns already visible in ICTD and participatory design, while others extend them through the particular sequence, constraints and design trace documented in this dissertation.

\section{What the Researcher Learned}

One of the strongest lessons of this research is that designing with people is not the same as collecting requirements from them. Requirements appear differently depending on what participants are able to see, imagine and experience. In Cycle 1, participants could speak from their existing experience and create simple representations. Once a PowerPoint prototype existed, they could identify problems that had not emerged in abstract discussion. Once the working artefact existed, new questions arose about use, generated documents, notifications, access and expectations. Each increase in concreteness made different knowledge available.

The study also showed that participation is shaped by the research setting before anyone begins a design activity. Group size matters. Organisational authority matters. Language matters. Familiarity with design methods matters. The possibility that a participant may obtain work and disappear from the research matters. These are not peripheral logistical details; they affect the evidence that a participatory method can produce.

Another lesson concerns restraint. The process of building an artefact can create momentum towards preserving it. Once software exists, it is easy to interpret every new problem as a reason to add another feature. The findings of this study suggest the opposite discipline: sometimes the most important design decision is to identify where the technology should stop. The same is true of methodology. More participation, more iterations and more features are not automatically better. Their value depends on what they contribute and what they demand from the people involved.

Finally, the researcher's role changed through this retrospective analysis. Rather than presenting the researcher as a neutral conduit through which participant ideas became software, the dissertation recognises synthesis itself as an act of design. The researcher interpreted, selected, prioritised and implemented. Making those decisions visible strengthens rather than weakens the claim to co-design because it makes clear where participant influence occurred and where researcher responsibility remained.

\section{Limitations}

The findings of this study should be understood within several limitations. The research was conducted through two Job Readiness organisations in Cape Town and was qualitative and situated. The findings are therefore not intended to represent all unemployed South Africans or all employment-support contexts. The value of the study lies instead in the detailed account of how barriers, participant contributions and design decisions interacted within these settings.

Participant continuity was limited. Different people contributed at different stages, which broadened the range of perspectives entering the artefact but reduced the possibility of tracing the same individuals across the entire process. The Cycle 1 workshops also differed considerably in size, meaning that the form and depth of participation were not identical across the two sites.

The study further relied on researcher synthesis and technical development. Participants influenced the artefact but did not exercise equal control over all design decisions. This has been treated explicitly as part of the analysis rather than concealed behind the term co-design.

Employers were not included. Their absence limits the claims that can be made about the viability of a platform intended ultimately to connect job-seekers with employment opportunities. Future investigation of employer requirements, trust, adoption and recruitment practices is therefore necessary.

The two-week deployment was also too short to assess continued use, sustainability or longer-term employment outcomes. Although one participant obtained employment during the deployment, the study cannot establish that the artefact caused the outcome. A longer deployment would be needed to investigate applications, interviews, employment progression and continued engagement with the service.

Finally, vacancy retrieval and matching were manually simulated. The study therefore does not provide a technical evaluation of automated vacancy retrieval or matching. This is an important limitation because the human judgement used during deployment would need either to remain part of a future service or to be translated into a separately evaluated automated process.

\section{Recommendations and Future Work}

The findings point first to the need for employer participation in subsequent work. Future co-design should examine what employers need from job-seeker profiles, which forms of skills or experience they consider credible, how they search for workers, what information requires verification and what would motivate them to adopt an employment-support service. Importantly, this work should not allow employer requirements simply to override those of job-seekers. The tensions between the two sides are themselves part of the design problem.

A future implementation should also investigate automated vacancy retrieval and matching. The manual process used in this study provides a description of the intended service, but not evidence of algorithmic performance. Future evaluation would need to assess relevance, false matches, missed opportunities, currentness of vacancies and possible disadvantages to people whose skills are not well represented through conventional qualifications or employment histories.

A longer deployment is needed to examine how the system behaves once novelty has passed and the researcher is no longer closely involved. Such work could investigate whether profiles remain current, whether job-seekers continue using the service, which forms of support remain necessary, whether notifications lead to applications and interviews, and whether resulting opportunities fit the person's skills and circumstances.

Sustainability should likewise be designed rather than assumed. The earlier dissertation proposed possibilities such as employer-supported funding and other revenue mechanisms, but these were not empirically evaluated. Future work should therefore co-design any sustainability model with the organisations and people expected to carry its costs. Maintaining the service would involve more than hosting software; it would include maintaining vacancies, supporting users, managing communication costs, protecting information and sustaining relationships with employers.

Future work should also continue investigating how job-seekers establish trust and reputation without excluding those who have not previously been given opportunities. Referrals, references or verified skills may provide alternatives to a simple rating score, but each mechanism introduces its own assumptions about whose previous work is visible and whose is not. These mechanisms therefore require further co-design rather than being added as straightforward features.

The original recommendations concerning job-finding guidance, language support, low-cost communication mechanisms and multimedia also remain possible directions. However, the lesson of this dissertation is that such features should not be developed simply because they appear useful. Each should begin with the barrier it is intended to address and should be tested with the people whose resources and practices determine whether it is useful.

Finally, future research should treat the methodology itself as something that can be iterated. Researchers working with populations where continuity cannot be assumed should plan for participant turnover, document previous design decisions and consider how newcomers are introduced to an evolving artefact. They should also distinguish participant Make activities from researcher-led development and make the translation from participant contribution to design decision visible. This traceability provides stronger evidence of participation than the co-design label alone.

\section{Final Reflection}

At the beginning of this research, the question appeared to be whether ICT could help unemployed job-seekers find work. By the end, the question had become more careful.

Technology can shorten some distances. It can carry a vacancy to a person who might otherwise spend money travelling to search for it. It can preserve information so that a CV does not have to be recreated each time. It can offer different communication routes and help make skills more visible. These are modest interventions when placed beside the scale of unemployment, but they are not meaningless ones when transport money, data, time and access are scarce.

Co-design can also shorten a different kind of distance: the distance between the assumptions of a designer and the lived experience of the person for whom a system is intended. In this study, participants corrected qualification categories, challenged reputation mechanisms, altered representations, expressed communication preferences and showed that an apparently simple problem such as ``finding a job'' contained many smaller problems. Yet co-design did not make everyone equal, nor did it remove the researcher's authority. It instead created moments in which different forms of knowledge could meet and, at their strongest, change the direction of the artefact.

This is perhaps the most important lesson of the three cycles. The artefact became more concrete from paper to PowerPoint to working software, but the research questions became more nuanced at the same time. Each design step revealed not only what could be built, but also what should be questioned. The large workshop raised questions about what participation means when inclusion increases but depth decreases. Participant turnover raised questions about whether continuity should always be demanded. The working artefact raised questions about the invisible human work behind apparently automated services. The emergence of the employer as a missing stakeholder showed that the people required to understand a problem are not always the same people required to sustain its proposed solution.

The dissertation therefore ends with two related propositions. A participatory methodology should not be preserved simply because it was selected at the beginning of a study, and a technological intervention should not be preserved simply because it was successfully built. Both must remain answerable to context, evidence and the people whose lives they are intended to affect.

Khanya Job Network was therefore not the final answer produced by this research. It was a means through which the questions became clearer. Through it, the study learned where technology may help, where people remain necessary, where participation becomes difficult, and where design must recognise its own limits. The contribution of this dissertation lies in that boundary: not in claiming that technology can overcome unemployment, but in showing how co-design can help identify the parts of job-seeking that can be changed, the people who need to participate in changing them, and the point at which responsible design must recognise that some problems lie beyond the artefact.
