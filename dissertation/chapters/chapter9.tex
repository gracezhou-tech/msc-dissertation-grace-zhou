\chapter{Conclusion}

\begin{chapterintro}
This chapter returns to where the dissertation began: job seekers describing the practical difficulties involved in looking for work, such as finding information, paying to search, preparing employment documents, representing qualifications and skills, accessing ICT and communicating with potential employers. From those accounts, the research moved through paper prototypes, a PowerPoint prototype, a working web artefact and finally a two week deployment. As the artefact became more concrete, the questions also became more precise. The study was no longer only asking what technology could be built, but which parts of job searching could reasonably be supported through ICT, what forms of participation could shape that technology, and where the limits of both the methodology and the artefact should be recognised.

This final chapter returns explicitly to the five research questions. It summarises what was found across the three cycles, identifies the contributions made by the study, distinguishes new contributions from findings that reinforce earlier work, reflects on what was learned through the research process, and sets out the limitations and future directions. The conclusion that emerges is deliberately bounded. Co-design can bring the experience of job seekers into technological design, and ICT can reduce some of the effort and cost surrounding job searching. Neither should be expected to carry the full weight of unemployment.
\end{chapterintro}

\section{Returning to the Research Problem}

The research sought to understand ICT use and barriers surrounding job searching among the low-skilled participants in the study and to explore, through co-design, technologies and design requirements that could support their search for work. Three research cycles were undertaken at Afrika Tikkun and The Zanokhanyo Network. Cycle 1 concentrated on understanding existing practices, barriers and participant ideas. Cycle 2 made the proposed intervention more concrete through one-on-one PowerPoint walkthroughs and refinement. Cycle 3 allowed participants to interact with the resulting working artefact and experience a two week vacancy-notification service.

Across these cycles, the study found that unemployment and job searching should not be treated as the same design problem. Whether enough suitable jobs exist is shaped by economic, institutional and labour-market conditions that an application cannot directly change. Searching for available work, however, contains smaller points of friction that can sometimes be redesigned. Information can be made easier to reach, qualifications and skills can be represented more accurately, repeated document preparation can be reduced, communication can be adapted to available resources, and vacancies can potentially be filtered before a job seeker spends time and money investigating them.

The value of the intervention therefore lies not in demonstrating a technological solution to unemployment, but in identifying which parts of the job searching process were open to technological support, which required continued human or organisational assistance, and which remained beyond the reach of the artefact.


%================================================
\section{What We Found Across the Three Cycles}

The study found that the difficulties experienced by participants were layered. Some related to employment itself, including insufficient suitable work, required qualifications, lack of experience, working conditions and employer decisions. Others related to the practical process of searching, including transport, vacancy information, CV preparation and communication. A further group specifically affected the use of ICT, including connectivity costs, access to devices, familiarity with online job searching, language and the ability to use digital systems independently.

This distinction matters because different barriers require different responses. A person who cannot afford mobile data and a person who does not hold a qualification required for a vacancy do not have the same problem, even though both problems may prevent an online application. Improving connectivity may help the first person use a digital service, but it cannot give the second person a missing qualification. Similarly, improving vacancy matching may reduce irrelevant search results but cannot create additional jobs.

The research also showed that relevant information can matter more than the quantity of information available. Participants did not simply need more vacancies. They needed opportunities that corresponded sufficiently with their qualifications, skills and circumstances to justify spending limited time and resources investigating them. The design problem therefore developed from access to information towards access to information that was useful to the individual job seeker.

Representation consequently became important. Formal qualification categories did not capture every skill that participants possessed, while the omission of N4, N5 and N6 qualifications from an early prototype demonstrated how a small data-design decision could make a person's actual qualification invisible. Practical skills and previous experience also needed to be represented, while self-description exposed the difficulty of translating personal strengths into formal recruitment language.

The study also reinforced the importance of application-document support. CV preparation was already part of the wider Job Readiness environment, and the working artefact subsequently generated CVs and cover letters from participant information. During Cycle 3, several participants specifically appreciated the CV format, while another valued the cover letter as a concise introduction. Reusable documents were therefore not merely technical additions. They responded to repeated work associated with preparing and presenting employment applications.

Communication was similarly contextual. SMS and email were useful for different reasons because participants differed in access and communication practices. The deeper finding was not that either channel was permanently superior, but that the intervention needed to communicate through routes that job seekers could actually use. Densmore et al.'s description of braided communications captures this well: effective communication can rely on several co-existing and mutually reinforcing channels rather than one universal technology \cite{densmore2013}. The contemporary rise of WhatsApp does not invalidate the historical SMS finding; it shows how the particular channels in that braid can evolve.

Finally, the study found that people remained part of the employment-support system. Trainers, NGO resources and other forms of assistance supported participants with computers, CV preparation, job readiness and interpretation. The presence of technology did not automatically remove the value of these resources. Later Southern African research likewise shows that a digital opportunity may exist while devices, connectivity, language, skills and human mediation still determine whether it is practically accessible \cite{keskinen2022}.

\section{Answering the Research Questions}

The five research questions are answered directly below. These answers are bounded by the qualitative and situated nature of the study, but each is supported by evidence accumulated through the three research cycles.


%------------------------------------------------
\subsection{RQ1: In what ways are the low-skilled using existing ICTs to find jobs?}

The participants used ICT as one part of a wider job searching environment rather than as their only route to employment. In the initial interviews, personal networks were prominent, with all four interviewed job seekers indicating that they sought work through friends while only one reported using a mobile phone to find work. Across the wider study, participants also used phones, social media, internet-based resources, email and computers available through NGOs or other shared locations. By Cycle 3, all participants who completed the final evaluation reported using the internet, although the ways in which they obtained access differed, including phones, a tablet, NGO computers and internet cafés.

RQ1 therefore shows that ICT use was mixed, uneven and embedded within wider social and physical job searching practices. Participants did not move neatly from door-to-door or network-based searching into a completely online model. They combined friends, physical searching, organisational resources and personal technologies according to what was available to them. Existing ICTs were therefore already part of participants' job searching practices, but the level of access to and familiarity with those ICTs differed.


%------------------------------------------------
\subsection{RQ2: What are the barriers to the use of ICTs for job searching for the low-skilled?}

The study found that barriers specifically affecting ICT-supported job searching included the cost of connectivity, inconsistent access to suitable internet-enabled devices, differences in familiarity with online job searching, uneven use of email, language difficulties and, for some participants, a need for assistance with computers or digital forms. One participant explained that using a phone to search cost money, while another participant without an internet-enabled personal device relied on the NGO or an internet café. A further participant struggled to enter information into the artefact because of limited English proficiency.

These barriers are distinct from, although sometimes connected to, wider employment barriers. A shortage of suitable jobs, lack of required experience, insufficient qualifications, transport to a workplace, wages and employer hiring decisions affect employment prospects but are not in themselves barriers to using ICT. Limited finances may affect both categories because the same person may struggle to afford transport as well as data, while language can affect both digital interaction and employability.

Later South African research suggests that these ICT access concerns remain relevant. Mangadi and Petersen identify connectivity, data costs, digital skills, usability and facilitating conditions as important to the acceptance and use of a contemporary data-free job search application \cite{mangadi2024}. Their work was published after this fieldwork and is used here to test the continuing relevance of the earlier findings rather than to explain decisions made during the original study.

RQ2 therefore shows that digitising employment information does not automatically make it accessible. ICT-supported job searching needs to minimise assumptions about continuous connectivity, device ownership, digital familiarity, language and complete independence of use. At the same time, removing ICT barriers cannot remove the wider employment barriers surrounding them.


%------------------------------------------------
\subsection{RQ3: Which technology or technologies are appropriate for low-skilled job seekers to find jobs?}

The study did not identify one universally appropriate technology. Instead, the findings support a combination of technologies whose suitability depends on the job searching task and the resources available to the individual job seeker.

For the participants in this study, a lightweight web-based artefact was appropriate for functions requiring richer interaction and information, including profile creation, representation of qualifications and skills, generation of CVs and cover letters, employment guidance and the organisation of information for vacancy matching. However, a web-only approach would have assumed regular internet access, a suitable device and sufficient digital familiarity, conditions that were not equally present across participants.

SMS and email therefore provided complementary communication channels. They allowed vacancy information to reach participants according to their circumstances without requiring repeated visits to the web application. SMS was particularly useful where regular data access or email use could not be assumed, while email could carry richer information and longer documents. Human and organisational support also remained appropriate where language, access or digital familiarity made fully independent use difficult.

This combination is consistent with Densmore et al.'s concept of braided communications \cite{densmore2013}. Rather than looking for one channel to replace all others, appropriate communication can emerge through several channels used together, with the strengths of one compensating for limitations in another.

RQ3 is therefore answered through a combination rather than a single technology: a lightweight web-based employment-support platform, alternative notification channels selected according to user circumstances, and continued access to human support where required. This is a contextual answer. The research does not claim that this combination is universally optimal for every low-skilled job seeker.

The available communication technologies have subsequently changed. WhatsApp is now widely used in South Africa and would be an important contemporary option to investigate, particularly for job seekers who already use smartphones and can access affordable or public Wi-Fi. WhatsApp could complement or, for some users, replace SMS while allowing richer messages, links and documents. This does not retrospectively change what was appropriate during the fieldwork. It reinforces the underlying finding that the appropriate technologies should be selected according to the resources and communication practices available to job seekers at the time.


%------------------------------------------------
\subsection{RQ4: What are the salient design requirements for ICTs used to assist job seekers in job finding?}

Using the understanding of salience established in Chapter 1, the study identified several design requirements that were strongly supported across the research process.

A central requirement was support for more than one affordable communication channel. Participant concerns about data and email during Cycle 2 were translated into SMS and email alternatives and evaluated during Cycle 3. The requirement should therefore be understood more broadly than the technologies used at the time: communication needs to adapt to the resources available to the job seeker.

Another requirement was reusable CV and cover-letter support. The need for assistance with employment documents was visible within the Job Readiness environment, the functions were implemented in the artefact, and participants later responded positively to the generated documents. The requirement therefore moved from an observed difficulty through implementation to participant evaluation.

The study also identified a need for access to relevant rather than merely numerous vacancy information. Participants needed employment information, but later feedback demonstrated that a vacancy service could still create unnecessary work if it supplied jobs for which the person did not qualify. Profile information and vacancy filtering therefore need to work together.

A further requirement was appropriate representation of qualifications, practical skills and employment experience. Participant identification of the missing N4, N5 and N6 categories materially altered the prototype and demonstrated that locally meaningful categories are necessary if a system is to represent job seekers accurately.

Support where fully independent ICT use is difficult was also important. Assistance with computers, language and employment preparation formed part of the wider research environment, while later use of shared facilities and difficulties entering information reinforced the continuing role of support. The requirement is not compulsory mediation. It is that a system should not exclude people who require assistance at particular points.

Finally, job seekers need sufficient control over self-representation. An employment-support system may help structure a profile, CV or cover letter, but the information should remain accurate and recognisable to the person it represents. Assistance should strengthen rather than silently replace the job seeker's voice.

RQ4 therefore contributes more than a list of possible features. The requirements can be traced through the study from participant experience or observed practice into design decisions and, in several cases, into subsequent participant evaluation.


%------------------------------------------------
\subsection{RQ5: What are the trade-offs to be made when performing co-design with low-skilled, unemployed or marginalised individuals from NGOs?}

The study identified several methodological trade-offs. The first was between breadth and depth. Large workshops made it possible to include many participants and collect a broad range of ideas within limited time, but reduced the researcher's ability to follow individual reasoning. Smaller and one-on-one interactions provided greater depth but necessarily involved fewer participants at one time.

A second trade-off concerned continuity and participant burden. Maintaining the same people through several cycles could have strengthened longitudinal engagement and familiarity with the design process. However, unemployed participants had legitimate competing demands, including active job searching, part-time employment and family responsibilities. Designing the research around perfect continuity could therefore place the convenience of the methodology above the circumstances of the participants.

A third trade-off concerned intermediary support and intermediary influence. Trainers helped create access, encouraged participation and contributed knowledge gained through supporting job seekers. At the same time, their organisational authority could change the research setting, as occurred when the intended small workshop became a much larger group activity. Intermediary expertise was valuable but should not replace first-hand information from job seekers.

A fourth trade-off concerned open-ended ideation and concrete critique. Early paper prototyping created space for participants to represent ideas but was unfamiliar to some participants and produced relatively high-level representations. The PowerPoint prototype provided a more concrete starting point and allowed participants to identify missing or undesirable features, but it also meant that the researcher had already exercised greater influence over the form of the proposed solution.

A final trade-off concerned participant influence and researcher responsibility. Participants did not program the artefact and did not exercise equal control over every technical decision. The researcher had to combine contributions, assess feasibility and implement the system. Meaningful co-design therefore depended on making the movement from participant contribution to design decision visible rather than pretending that authority was equally distributed.

RQ5 consequently shows that co-design with unemployed or marginalised participants requires flexibility. Different methods create different possibilities for participation, and the method should be changed where the conditions of participation change. These trade-offs do not demonstrate that co-design failed. They help explain what co-design required in this particular context.

\section{Contributions of the Dissertation}

The contribution of this dissertation does not depend on claiming that every finding is entirely new. Some findings reinforce concerns already present in participatory design and ICTD literature, while others arise more specifically from the way the three cycles, artefact development and methodological difficulties unfolded in this study. Together they make contributions to both co-design methodology and employment-support ICT.


%------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Participation Through Influence Rather Than Equal Technical Production}

The first contribution is an empirically grounded way of interpreting participation. Existing co-design literature recognises that participation and making extend beyond software programming \cite{sanders2008,sanders2014}. The present study shows what that distinction looks like in practice. Job seekers contributed experiences, concerns, representations, critiques and design suggestions, while the researcher retained responsibility for synthesis and implementation.

The contribution is therefore to evaluate participation through traceable influence. Qualification categories changed, communication alternatives were retained, the rating mechanism was questioned, self-description was expanded and aspects of presentation were influenced by participants. These changes provide evidence that participant knowledge entered the artefact even though participants did not develop the software themselves.

The relevant question is not whether every participant performed every design activity, but whether the method created conditions in which their experiential knowledge could materially influence the design. This framing also makes researcher authority easier to discuss honestly. Co-design does not require pretending that technical responsibility disappeared. It requires showing where participant knowledge changed the design and where researcher judgement remained.


%------------------------------------------------
\subsection{\texorpdfstring{\tme}{Tell-Make-Enact} Across Rather Than Within Research Cycles}

A second contribution is the interpretation of \tme{} as a process that crossed the boundaries of the numbered research cycles. Cycle 1 contained Tell and participant Make, while the related Enact occurred when the resulting PowerPoint prototype was experienced during Cycle 2. The feedback generated through that enactment produced another Tell and informed further Make, eventually leading to the working artefact enacted in Cycle 3.

The contribution is not a replacement \tme{} framework. It is a situated demonstration that methodological loops and administrative research cycles do not necessarily share the same boundaries. An idea can begin during one interaction, be synthesised between participant encounters and only later become sufficiently concrete to enact.


%------------------------------------------------
\subsection{When to Fail a Methodology}

A third contribution emerges from the methodological difficulties encountered during the study. Participant turnover, changing group size, differences in readiness, intermediary authority and the emergence of new stakeholders initially appear as limitations. Considered together, however, they suggest a broader principle: a participatory methodology should itself remain open to redesign.

To ``fail'' a methodology in this sense does not mean abandoning participation. It means recognising when a particular methodological arrangement is no longer producing the form of participation intended and changing that arrangement. Moving from broad workshops towards focused one-on-one prototype interaction can therefore be understood as methodological adaptation rather than a retreat from co-design.

This argument parallels Densmore's account of failing a technology when continuation no longer appropriately serves the problem \cite{densmore2012}. Methodologies, like technologies, should remain answerable to context rather than being preserved simply because they were selected at the beginning.


%------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Distinguishing Designable, Supportable and Structural Barriers}

A fourth contribution is the distinction between barriers that can be directly designed for in part, barriers that can be supported through ICT but continue to require other forms of assistance, and barriers that remain predominantly structural.

Vacancy discovery, notifications and reusable document generation fall largely into the first category. Language, self-representation, interpretation and aspects of digital skill fall into the second. Job scarcity, employer decisions, wages and wider labour-market conditions fall predominantly into the third.

This distinction discourages the conversion of every social or employment difficulty into a software requirement. It provides a way of asking what relationship a particular barrier actually has to technology before deciding that another technological feature should be built.


%------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Contextual Appropriateness Requires a Combination of Technologies}

A fifth contribution arises directly from RQ3. Technology appropriateness was not located in one device, platform or communication channel. The answer that emerged from a question about which technology or technologies were appropriate was itself plural.

The web artefact, SMS, email and human or organisational support performed different functions. Their usefulness depended on the job searching activity, the amount and form of information involved and the participant's access conditions. Densmore et al.'s braided communications provide a useful conceptual explanation for this finding \cite{densmore2013}. Technologies can coexist and support one another rather than competing until one becomes the universal solution.

The contemporary emergence of WhatsApp as another plausible channel further reinforces this contribution. The exact technologies may change while the design principle remains stable. Appropriate employment-support should therefore be capable of evolving with users' communication practices rather than binding the intervention permanently to the technology that was dominant when the system was first designed.


%------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Stakeholders Can Emerge as the Artefact Evolves}

A sixth contribution concerns stakeholder identification. Job seekers and trainers were appropriate participants for understanding the original job searching problem, but employers became increasingly consequential as the intervention developed into a platform concerned with matching and job seeker representation.

This suggests that stakeholder identification should be revisited during a co-design project rather than treated as complete at the beginning. The people required to understand a problem may not be identical to those required to make an emerging intervention work. The missing employer therefore represents both a limitation of this study and a methodological finding.


%------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Making the Hidden Human Work of Automation Visible}

The manually simulated vacancy-matching process provides another contribution. Participants experienced the service outcome of receiving selected vacancies, but human judgement produced those selections. The deployment therefore makes visible the work that an apparently automated service would eventually need either to retain as human work or reproduce computationally.

Automating that judgement is not merely the final programming step for an otherwise validated function. It introduces questions concerning how job seekers and vacancies are represented, how relevance is determined, whether appropriate opportunities are missed, whether unsuitable opportunities are selected and how current the source information remains. The study therefore contributes a clearer distinction between evaluating a proposed service experience and evaluating the automated mechanism that might later provide that service.

\section{What This Study Confirms and Extends}

Not every worthwhile research contribution needs to contradict previous work. Several findings in this study reinforce concerns already documented in participatory design and ICTD and demonstrate their relevance within this South African employment-support setting.

The importance of combining communication channels aligns particularly strongly with Densmore et al.'s account of braided communications \cite{densmore2013}. The present study similarly found that no one channel met every need. Web access enabled richer interaction, SMS provided short notifications under constrained connectivity, email supported longer information and documents, and human or organisational channels continued to matter.

The continuing significance of data, connectivity, usability and digital skills is reinforced by Mangadi and Petersen's later research on a South African data-free job search application \cite{mangadi2024}. Their findings suggest that reducing data costs can remove an important barrier without removing all of the conditions affecting actual use. This strengthens the present study's conclusion that technology should be assessed in relation to the entire job searching activity rather than according to the availability of an application alone.

The continued role of support is similarly consistent with the findings of Keskinen et al. \cite{keskinen2022}. Their Southern African study illustrates that digital livelihood opportunities may remain inaccessible where the surrounding capabilities and infrastructure are absent. The present research extends this concern into job searching by showing how NGO spaces, trainers, computer access and assistance with employment documents formed part of the practical environment in which the artefact could be used.

The methodological difficulties also align with more recent South African work on co-design readiness \cite{till2026}. Because that literature post-dates the present fieldwork, it provides a retrospective interpretive lens rather than an explanation of the original design choices. It helps make sense of why readiness, familiarity, trust and the conditions surrounding participation need to be considered when evaluating whether a particular co-design activity succeeded.

The dissertation therefore contributes partly through confirmation and partly through extension. Existing concerns about access, context, intermediation and participation become more specific when their movement from participant experience to design choice and later enactment can be followed across an empirical process.


%================================================
\section{What Was Learned Through the Research}

One lesson from the three cycles is that requirements become visible differently as an artefact becomes more concrete. During Cycle 1, participants could speak from their existing experience and represent possible responses. Once the PowerPoint prototype existed, they could identify missing categories and challenge particular design decisions that had not become visible through open discussion. Once the working artefact existed, new questions concerning use, generated documents, notifications, connectivity and participant expectations became visible. Each increase in concreteness enabled a different form of knowledge.

A second lesson is that the conditions surrounding participation are themselves part of the methodology. Group size matters, organisational authority matters, language matters, familiarity with design activities matters, and the possibility that a participant may obtain employment and leave the programme matters. These should not be treated merely as logistical disturbances because they shape the evidence that a participatory method can produce.

The research also taught the importance of restraint. Once an artefact has been built, there is a temptation to interpret every remaining problem as another feature to add. The findings of this study suggest a different discipline. Some problems require better software, some require human or organisational support, and some require changes outside the technological system altogether. Recognising what not to automate can therefore be as important as identifying what can be automated.

Finally, the researcher's role became more visible through this analysis. The researcher did not simply receive participant wishes and convert them mechanically into code. Synthesis involved interpretation, selection, prioritisation and technical judgement. Recognising that process does not weaken the co-design account. It makes clearer what participants contributed and where researcher responsibility remained.

\section{Limitations}

The study was qualitative and situated within two Job Readiness organisations in Cape Town. The findings are therefore not intended to represent all unemployed or low-skilled South African job seekers. Their value lies instead in the detailed account of how participants, context, design methods and technological decisions interacted within these settings.

Participant continuity was limited. Different people contributed at different stages, which broadened the range of perspectives entering the study but reduced the possibility of tracing one stable participant group from initial problem identification through deployment. The Cycle 1 workshops also differed substantially in size, meaning that the depth and form of participation were not identical across the two sites.

The researcher retained methodological, analytical and technical responsibility. Participants materially influenced the artefact but did not exercise equal control over every design decision. The dissertation therefore does not claim that every aspect of the resulting software was directly co-produced by participants.

Employers were not included as participants. Their absence limits conclusions concerning the employer-facing side of the platform, employer adoption, trust, recruitment requirements and how tensions between employer and job seeker requirements should be resolved.

The deployment lasted only two weeks. It could therefore provide evidence about initial interaction, utility and participant perspectives, but not sustained use, organisational sustainability or long-term employment outcomes. One participant obtained employment during the deployment, but this outcome cannot be causally attributed to the artefact.

Finally, vacancy retrieval and matching were manually simulated. The study does not provide a technical evaluation of automated vacancy retrieval or matching. Claims concerning the usefulness of receiving customised vacancy information should therefore remain separate from claims about whether an automated system could produce those matches accurately, fairly and sustainably.

\section{Recommendations and Future Work}

The strongest next step is to include employers or recruiters in future co-design. Future work should investigate what employers require from job seeker profiles, which qualifications and practical skills they consider credible, how they identify suitable workers, what would motivate them to use the service and how employer requirements can be balanced against the interests and dignity of job seekers. Employer participation should extend rather than replace the job seeker centred perspective established in this study.

Automated vacancy retrieval and matching also requires separate investigation. The researcher's manual process provides a description of the intended service but not evidence of algorithmic effectiveness. A future implementation would need to examine relevance, omitted vacancies, unsuitable matches, the currentness of vacancy information and the possibility that people whose abilities are difficult to represent through conventional qualifications or work histories could be disadvantaged.

A longer deployment would allow investigation of what occurs after initial novelty has passed and when the researcher is no longer closely involved. Future work could examine whether profiles remain current, whether job seekers continue using the service, which forms of support remain necessary, whether notifications lead to applications and interviews, and whether resulting employment corresponds sufficiently with the person's skills and circumstances.

Future work should also revisit the communication braid. SMS and email were appropriate historical options within the present deployment, but a contemporary implementation should investigate WhatsApp alongside them. The choice should still depend on actual access circumstances rather than assuming that smartphone ownership or internet connectivity is universal. Where public or free Wi-Fi is available, WhatsApp may reduce direct communication costs for some users; where connectivity is unreliable or absent, SMS may remain important. The design should therefore preserve channel flexibility as technologies and costs continue to change.

Sustainability needs to be designed as part of the intervention rather than treated solely as the cost of hosting software. Future work should identify who maintains vacancy information, supports job seekers, manages communication costs, maintains relationships with employers, protects personal information and remains accountable for the system. Any financial model should itself be investigated with the organisations and people expected to participate in it rather than being assumed from the outset.

Reputation and references also require further co-design. Employers may require evidence of reliability or previous performance, but a simple rating mechanism risks reinforcing disadvantage for people who have not yet received an opportunity to establish a work history. Alternative mechanisms such as references, verified practical skills or contextual evidence of experience should therefore be explored without making previous opportunity the gatekeeper for future opportunity.

Contemporary AI also creates new possibilities for CV drafting, cover letters, language support, vacancy summarisation and matching. However, the lessons from this dissertation remain relevant. An AI-generated representation is still dependent on the information provided to it, and automation can still create exclusion if categories, training data or matching criteria fail to represent the job seeker accurately. Future AI-supported employment tools should therefore preserve transparency, participant control and the possibility of human review rather than assuming that automation removes the need for support.

Finally, future co-design studies with similar populations should plan explicitly for participant turnover and methodological change. Researchers should document earlier design decisions so that new participants can understand what has already occurred, distinguish participant Make activities from researcher-led development, and continually examine whether the methods being used still create meaningful opportunities for influence.

\section{Final Reflection}

At the beginning of this research, the question appeared to be whether ICT could help low-skilled job seekers find work. By the end of the three cycles, the answer had become more careful.

Technology can shorten some distances. It can carry information about a vacancy to someone who might otherwise spend money travelling simply to discover whether an opportunity exists. It can preserve information so that a CV does not have to be recreated repeatedly. It can help make qualifications and practical skills visible and can reduce the amount of irrelevant information through which a job seeker must search. These interventions may appear modest beside the scale of unemployment, but they are not necessarily modest to a person for whom transport, data, printing and time are scarce resources.

Co-design can shorten another kind of distance: the distance between what a designer assumes and what a job seeker experiences. In this study, participants identified missing qualifications, challenged a reputation mechanism, influenced communication choices, altered representations and exposed difficulties that would have been easy to overlook from a purely technical perspective. Yet co-design did not make all participants equal, nor did it remove the researcher's authority. At its strongest, it created moments in which different forms of knowledge could meet and materially change what was subsequently designed.

The research also became more critical as the artefact became more concrete. The large workshop raised questions about what inclusion means when greater numbers reduce depth. Participant turnover raised questions about whether continuity should always be demanded. The working artefact exposed the human work hidden beneath a service intended eventually to be automated. The development of an employment platform exposed employers as stakeholders whose participation had not appeared as central at the beginning of the research.

Even the technologies themselves tell a similar story. SMS was valuable within the circumstances of the fieldwork, but communication practices have continued to change. WhatsApp, public Wi-Fi and newer digital services now alter what may be appropriate for some job seekers. The deeper contribution is therefore not the selection of one permanent technology, but the recognition that appropriate ICT should evolve with the resources and practices of the people using it. In this respect, the communication braid continues to change even when the need for accessible and reliable communication remains.

The dissertation therefore ends with two related propositions. A participatory methodology should not be preserved simply because it was selected at the beginning of a study, and a technological intervention should not be preserved simply because it was successfully built. Both must remain answerable to the people involved, the evidence produced and the context in which they are expected to operate.

Khanya Job Network is therefore not the final answer produced by this dissertation. It became a means through which the questions could be examined more closely. Through its design, refinement and deployment, the study identified where ICT may reduce particular frictions in job searching, where human support remains valuable, where co-design itself requires adaptation, and where responsible design must acknowledge that a problem lies beyond the reach of the artefact.

The contribution of this dissertation lies within that boundary. It does not claim that technology can overcome unemployment. It shows instead how co-design can help identify which parts of job searching are open to change, which technologies and forms of support may be appropriate for those parts, which requirements matter to the people using them, and where both methodology and technology must recognise their limits.
